Chapter 12: Synthetic Data & Distillation

RLHF began on the premise that only humans could write good answers and give reliable feedback. As models improved, "this assumption rapidly broke down". 1 2 Today the leading models "need synthetic data" for nearly every step: prompts, completions, preferences, filters and verification. 3 The chapter runs from the roles of synthetic data, through the math of on-policy distillation, to AI feedback, Constitutional AI and rubrics. Lambert calls the lecture "secretly a very dense lecture" with "a lot of math". 4

The chapter in five points

  1. Synthetic data won where models beat humans. SFT completions largely; preferences only partly; evaluation scoring, but not ground truth. 5 6
  2. "Distillation" means two things. The technical teacher-student method, and loosely, training on a stronger model's outputs. 7 8
  3. Offline distillation is SFT. Minimizing cross-entropy to a fixed teacher is minimizing forward KL. 9
  4. On-policy distillation samples from the student and scores each token with the teacher, which makes it a reverse KL that drops into RL as a per-token advantage. 10 11
  5. AI feedback is cheap but biased. Human data is "high-noise and low-bias", AI data "low-noise and high-bias". Constitutional AI and rubrics are two ways to steer it. 12 13 14

After this chapter you should be able to

  • Say where synthetic data replaced human data, and where it has not.
  • Derive why offline KD and SFT minimize forward KL, and on-policy distillation reverse KL.
  • Explain exposure bias, and why sampling from the student helps.
  • Write the per-token OPD advantage and say what makes it an estimate.
  • Describe the two kinds of data Constitutional AI generates, and how a rubric becomes an RL reward.

The roles of synthetic data

In Lecture 7: 00:31 · 01:32 · 05:40 · 06:41 · 07:41

The slides define synthetic data as "any training data used for an AI model that is generated in some part from another AI model", and add: "This is very broad!" 15 It is cheaper and faster to iterate on, which opened post-training experiments to far more people; 2 the slides add that speed is what enables "the RSI arguments we hear today". 16 When Lambert started the book there was "substantive debate"; now "synthetic data just does everything". 17

It needed a capability threshold. Llama 2 and GPT-3.5-Turbo were not reliable enough to generate or supervise data; LLM-as-a-judge ability emerged in the jump to GPT-4 class models. 18 Lambert dates the spread to 2023 and 2024, once open-weight models reached GPT-4 level. 19

Distillation with synthetic data

In Lecture 7: 08:43 · 09:14 · 09:45 · 10:15

The word comes from teacher-student knowledge distillation (KD), 7 but colloquially it means "using the outputs from a stronger model to train a smaller model". 8 30 Lambert expects the confusion to stay: "this will not go away". 31 In post-training, distillation is either a data engine for the whole pipeline or a way to transfer one skill, such as math or code. 32 Labs train large internal models, such as Claude Opus or Gemini Ultra, to make stronger models; open models distill from closed APIs, where curating prompts and filtering responses "is crucial". 33

Reasoning models changed this. Closed APIs stopped returning reasoning traces, so distillation moved to "more aggressive jailbreaking" or to open models whose full traces you can see. 34 35 The book's KD figure · the data-pipeline figure.

The path to on-policy distillation

In Lecture 7: 10:47 · 12:21 · 14:26 · 17:30 · 19:33

Teacher-student KD is back, driven by reasoning and agentic models: Qwen3, MiMo-V2-Flash, GLM-5 and DeepSeek-V4-Pro were trained with new forms of it. 36 The chapter says future editions may give distillation its own chapter. 37 Lambert's reaction while preparing: "all the math looks the same", expectations of reward and managing KLs. 38

1. Soft labels, per token

KD trains on soft labels, the teacher's whole distribution over next tokens, rather than a one-hot target. 39 Notation: $s$ is the prompt, $u=(u_1,\ldots,u_J)$ a teacher sequence, $\mathcal{V}$ the vocabulary, $q$ the teacher's next-token distribution and $p$ the student's. The letter $a$ is saved for tokens the student samples. 40 The slides call that split "the whole point of 'on-policy'". 41

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{WORD\text{-}KD}} = -\sum_{j=1}^{J}\sum_{k=1}^{|\mathcal{V}|} q(u_j = k \mid s, u_{<j})\,\log p(u_j = k \mid s, u_{<j})$$

At each position $j$, sum over every token $k$: the student is penalized for putting low probability where the teacher puts high probability. The text is a static corpus. 42 Kim & Rush called it "word-level"; read it as per-token. 40 Matching every token is cheap, the same cost as cross-entropy; matching whole sequences is the hard part. 43

2. Sequence-level KD: let the teacher write

Kim & Rush (2016) had the student learn from sequences the teacher generated. 44 Lambert calls generating from the teacher the paper's "key insight". 45 Summing over all sequences is intractable, so they take one beam-search output $\hat u$ as a point mass: 46

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SEQ\text{-}KD}}(s) = -\sum_{u \in \mathcal{U}} q(u \mid s)\log p(u \mid s) \;\approx\; -\log p(\hat{u} \mid s) = -\sum_{j=1}^{|\hat{u}|}\log p(\hat{u}_j \mid s, \hat{u}_{<j})$$

The teacher's probabilities vanish; what is left is plain next-token loss on the teacher's text. The chapter calls this offline KD, since the data is generated in advance. 47 DistilBERT and TinyBERT were offline KD too, though not sequence KD. 48

3. Offline KD is forward KL, which is SFT

$$H(q,p) = -\sum_z q(z)\log p(z) = \underbrace{-\sum_z q(z)\log q(z)}_{H(q)} + \underbrace{\sum_z q(z)\log\frac{q(z)}{p(z)}}_{D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p)}$$

$H(q)$ depends only on the fixed teacher, so minimizing cross-entropy is minimizing the forward KL from teacher to student, "the KL direction used by offline KD and SFT-like training". 9 The slides' conclusion: sequence-level KD "reduces to SFT on the teacher's generated text". 49

4. Exposure bias

The student trains on the teacher's prefixes $(s, u_{<t})$ but acts on its own $(s, a_{<t})$. That gap is exposure bias. 50 51 Offline KD can also push the student to overestimate the teacher's low-probability regions. 52

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KD}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{s,\, u \sim \pi_T}\sum_t D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\pi_T(\cdot\mid s,u_{<t})\,\|\,\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s,u_{<t})\big) \quad\text{vs.}\quad \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{eval}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{s,\, a \sim \pi_\theta}\,\ell_{\mathrm{task}}(s,a)$$

Imitation learning has a name for the fix. DAgger lets the learner act, then has an oracle say what it should have done. 53 If the learner errs with rate $\epsilon$ per step on the teacher's states, errors along a length-$L$ rollout can grow as $O(\epsilon L^2)$; 54 training on its own states cuts that to $O(\epsilon L)$. 55 For LLMs this "should be read as an analogy", since token losses are distributions, not 0-1 action errors. 56 The intuition holds: one odd token puts the prefix out of distribution, the model errs again, and on-policy training teaches recovery. 55

5. On-policy distillation is reverse KL

Sample $a \sim \pi_\theta$ from the student, let $s_t = (s, a_{<t})$, and score each visited state against the fixed teacher: 10

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{OPD}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{s,\, a \sim \pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s)}\sum_t D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s_t)\,\|\,\pi_T(\cdot\mid s_t)\big)$$

Because the expectation samples the student and the student sits on the left, this is reverse KL. 10 MiniLLM proposed the reverse KL inside a policy-gradient frame; Agarwal et al. ran on-policy KD concurrently. 57 Lambert says the Agarwal formulation (generalized knowledge distillation) is the more popular one, closer to what Gemma and Gemini use. 58 The models also had to get better before this was feasible. 59 Writing it as an expectation over the student's samples maps it onto RL infrastructure, which alternates generation and updates. 60

With two modes, the forward fit spreads to cover both, putting mass in the valley between them; the reverse fit sits on the larger mode and ignores the other. That is mass-covering against mode-seeking. 61 62 Tokens the teacher rates above the student get a positive advantage. 65

6. The KL as an RL advantage

Recent implementations go further: the negative per-token reverse-KL contribution becomes the advantage in an RL algorithm. 11 Lambert calls the 2025 Thinking Machines post "the canonical reference". 66

$$A_t^{\mathrm{OPD}} = \log \pi_T(a_t \mid s_t) - \log \pi_\theta(a_t \mid s_t)$$

One sampled token gives an unbiased estimate of the KL at that state; the advantage is its negative. 67 It looks like forward KL; what makes it reverse is sampling $a_t \sim \pi_\theta$, "not the sign". 68 It is dense, token-level feedback, possibly richer than a sparse verifiable reward, 65 and it can be combined with other advantages such as GRPO's group normalization. 69 Lambert notes "a lot of rumors, but not a lot of work" on interleaving it with outcome rewards. 70 The widget's sampling buttons show the estimate: single samples vary, and their mean approaches $-D_{\mathrm{KL}}$.

7. Many teachers, and self-distillation

Multi-teacher OPD (MOPD) uses specialist teachers, for math or code, or earlier checkpoints, each with a per-prompt weight $w_k(s)$ summing to 1: 71

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{MOPD}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{s,\, a \sim \pi_\theta}\sum_t \sum_k w_k(s)\, D_{\mathrm{KL}}\big(\pi_\theta(\cdot\mid s_t)\,\|\,\pi_{T_k}(\cdot\mid s_t)\big)$$

It lets a growing organization split the work: many groups build expert teachers for one final student, as for DeepSeek-V4 and MiMo-V2-Flash. 72 73 In the Finbarr Timbers conversation the student's trajectories are routed "to various expert models", 74 and implementing it is called "quite straightforward" in an RL framework. 75 The hard part is the teachers. NVIDIA's Nemotron paper, read out in the conversation, found teachers from "substantially different training pipelines cannot be effectively combined". 76 A paper recalled there suggests distilling from in-progress checkpoints in stages, "or else there's going to be too much divergence". 77

On-policy self-distillation (OPSD) is for the frontier, where no stronger teacher exists. The teacher is the same model with privileged information in its context. 78 79 Cursor's Composer 2.5: a judge checks RL trajectories for common bugs, inserts a hint when it finds one, and the distillation loss teaches the model to reach the fix unaided. 80 81 Lambert thinks this "needs a pretty strong base model", so academic work may struggle. 82

The code: SDPO

In the companion code: code/distillation/

The companion code implements SDPO, a form of on-policy self-distillation, on a string-reversal task small enough for one GPU. 89 One model plays both roles. The student pass sees only the question; the teacher pass sees the question plus a correct sibling answer from the same group of rollouts. 90 91 If no rollout in the group is correct, there is nothing to distill from and the prompt is skipped. 92 93 The loss keeps the top-$K$ logits plus one tail bucket for the rest of the mass, 94 and computes the reverse KL with the teacher detached: 95

        s_logits = model(
            input_ids=batch["s_ids"][sl],
            attention_mask=batch["s_mask"][sl],
            use_cache=False,
            logits_to_keep=A + 1,
        ).logits[:, :-1, :]
        s_topk, idx = s_logits.topk(self.kl_top_k, dim=-1)
        s_logp = s_topk - s_logits.logsumexp(dim=-1, keepdim=True)

        with torch.no_grad():
            t_logits = model(
                input_ids=batch["t_ids"][sl],
                attention_mask=batch["t_mask"][sl],
                use_cache=False,
                logits_to_keep=A + 1,
            ).logits[:, :-1, :]
            t_logp = t_logits.gather(-1, idx) - t_logits.logsumexp(dim=-1, keepdim=True)

        s_logp, t_logp = add_tail(s_logp), add_tail(t_logp)
        # Reverse KL = KL(student || teacher): target is the student, so the gradient
        # flows through s_logp while the teacher (input) stays detached.
        kl = F.kl_div(t_logp, s_logp, reduction="none", log_target=True).sum(-1)
        return (kl * batch["action_mask"][sl]).sum() / denom

F.kl_div(input, target) computes $\sum \text{target}\,(\log\text{target} - \text{input})$. With the student as target, that is $D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_\theta\|\pi_T)$, as the code's comment says. 95 The chapter suggests sweeping num_rollouts, kl_top_k and prompts_per_step. 96 The README's reference run, Qwen3-1.7B on one 24 GB GPU, took under 20 hours, with reward rising from about 0.55 to 0.8. 97 One practical warning: pick the demonstration at random, because picking the shortest "collapses training". 98 I have not run it.

AI feedback

In Lecture 7: 37:49 · 38:22 · 38:52 · 39:53 · 40:54 · 41:26

RLAIF uses AI to stand in for the human labeling step. It began with pairwise preferences. 99 The same judge infrastructure later scaled evaluation and enabled rubric rewards. 100 The cost gap drove adoption: as of 2026, a human preference label costs "$1 or higher (or even above $10 per prompt)", AI feedback under a cent. Human labor costs stay flat while models get cheaper per unit of quality. 101 102 103

Balancing AI and human feedback

Early RLAIF papers claimed AI feedback could fully replace humans, especially on chat. Later work found the best mix routes hard examples to humans and the rest to AI. 104 AI feedback dominates evaluation, but "will never fully replace human data, even for evaluation". 105 The advice: start with AI feedback, and expect to add human feedback as you scale. 106 Industry treats human data as "a substantial moat". 107 108 Lambert "would bet money" that labs have not fully dropped human preference data; 109 in the Finbarr Timbers conversation the guess is "likely some human preference data still in the loop". 110

Rule of thumb

Human data: high noise, low bias. Hard to collect and filter, but reliable once wrangled. AI data: low noise, high bias. Easy to start with, but with "tricky, unintended second-order effects" baked in. 12 The slides call it a colloquial rule he heard; 111 in the lecture he traces it to a conversation with John Schulman about what industry sees, who said "I don't know the answer" to whether Ai2 needed human preference data. 112

Should you train your own judge?

LLM judges are inconsistent and show self-preference bias, favoring their own outputs. 113 Lambert's example: "if you have a OpenAI judge, it'll prefer outputs from OpenAI models". 114 Dedicated judge models such as Prometheus exist but "are not widely adopted". 115 Repeated sampling, self-refinement or tournaments can improve judgments. 116 Usually you need not train your own judge, unless your task involves private information. 117 Judging is "one of these core competencies of the frontier models". 118

Constitutional AI

In Lecture 7: 42:28 · 42:59 · 43:30 · 44:02 · 44:33 · 45:05

CAI, which Anthropic uses for Claude, is "the earliest documented, large-scale use of synthetic data for RLHF training". 13 Its paper also coined RLAIF, causing years of confusion; the clean reading is that CAI kickstarted RLAIF. 119 The slides write it as "CAI ⊂ RLAIF". 120 They also warn the name now covers a constitution Anthropic still uses, though its methods "definitely ha[ve] shifted substantially". 121

  1. Critique and revise, for SFT. A constitution $\mathcal{C}$ lists principles such as "Is the answer encouraging violence?". 122 Repeatedly sample a principle $c_i$ and have the model revise its latest answer $y^i$ to fit it. Train on the prompt $x$ with the final answer $y^n$. 123
  2. AI preference labels, for RL. Give a feedback model the prompt, some principles and two answers labeled (A) and (B); it picks the better and more aligned one. 124 125 Early models read off which of the tokens A or B was more likely after "The answer is: "; today a generative reward model reasons first. 125

CAI is known for the second half, but the critique methods spread into data filtering and generation generally. 126 127 Lambert's own take on stage 1 is more hedged (see disagreements). Principles are sampled at random, so each is spread thinly across many data points. 128 Few extensions are documented as clear improvements. 129 Relatives include OpenAI's Model Spec and Deliberative Alignment, and Anthropic's updated constitution. 130 Lambert's own open replication work produced dialogue data between language models. 131

Rubrics: prompt-specific AI feedback

RLVR needs a verifier; most prompts do not have one. A rubric gives "nearly-verifiable criteria", so a model can generate several answers and RL toward the best. 14 A judge grades each answer against the rubric, "and then that grade is the reward signal in RL training". 132 They now bring "meaningful improvements" in scientific reasoning and factuality. 133

Check yourself

Why is minimizing cross-entropy to a fixed teacher the same as minimizing forward KL?

$H(q,p) = H(q) + D_{\mathrm{KL}}(q\|p)$, and $H(q)$ does not depend on the student. 9

What is exposure bias, and how does on-policy distillation address it?

The student trains on teacher prefixes but acts on its own. OPD samples from the student and supervises the states it actually visits, so it learns to recover. 51 55

$\log\pi_T - \log\pi_\theta$ looks like forward KL. Why is OPD reverse KL?

Because the token is sampled from the student, $a_t \sim \pi_\theta$. The sampling, not the sign, sets the direction. 68

The teacher gives a sampled token log-probability −1.2 and the student −2.0. What is the OPD advantage, and what happens to the token?

$-1.2 - (-2.0) = +0.8$. Positive, so the student is pushed to make the token more likely. 65

Why does per-token KD usually require a shared tokenizer?

The supervision is a per-token distribution from the other model, so both must predict over the same tokens. 84

What two kinds of data does Constitutional AI generate?

Revised answers for SFT, by critiquing against sampled principles; and AI preference labels for RL, judged against principles. 122 124

Human labels vs. AI labels: which is high-bias?

AI labels: low noise, high bias. Human labels: high noise, low bias. 12

Why write rubrics per prompt, when it costs more?

For quality and robustness to over-optimization; teams soften the cost with a per-domain starting rubric. 138 137

Agent notes

agent note The KL widget. Not Lambert. One state, ten tokens. The student is a discretized bell curve with a peak and a spread; the fit buttons search that family for the lowest forward or reverse KL. Sampling draws tokens from the student and averages $\log\pi_T - \log\pi_\theta$, whose expectation is exactly $-D_{\mathrm{KL}}(\pi_\theta\|\pi_T)$. 67 A real LLM student is not restricted to one peak, so the gap between the two fits is exaggerated here.

agent note The rubric score. Not Lambert. Points are the weights of met criteria plus the pitfall weight if the mistake is made. The reward is points divided by the most positive points available, floored at 0, and set to 0 if the gate is on and Essential is missed. The chapter gives categories and weights, 143 142 and the slides say subcomponents "contribute to the score", 136 but neither gives an aggregation rule.

agent note The DAgger bounds are big-O. $O(\epsilon L^2)$ against $O(\epsilon L)$ hides constants, so at $L = 1{,}000$ "a thousand times worse" is the scaling, not a prediction. The chapter itself calls it an analogy for LLMs. 56
