Chapter 9: Rejection Sampling

Rejection sampling generates many completions, keeps the ones a reward model scores highest, and fine-tunes on them with the ordinary SFT loss. 1 It is one of the most used and least documented methods in preference fine-tuning. 2 Lambert is candid: "I do not have good intuitions for what makes or breaks a good rejection sampling run". 3 So this chapter is short, and the open questions matter as much as the method.

The chapter in five points

  1. Generate, score, select, fine-tune. Sample from the current model, filter with a reward model, and run SFT on what survives. 1 4
  2. Two ways to select. The best completion for each prompt, or the top $K$ across all prompts. 5 6
  3. The settings are simple but matter. Temperature 0.7 to 1.0, 10 to 30 or more completions per prompt, and a good reward model. 7 8 9
  4. Best-of-N is the same without training. Pick the best completion at inference time; the model does not change. 10 11
  5. Widely used, poorly understood. WebGPT, Anthropic's HH agent and Llama 2 used it, 12 yet which prompts, which reward model and when to apply it are "not well documented". 13

After this chapter you should be able to

  • Lay out the four stages and the $M \times N$ reward matrix.
  • Apply both selection rules to a reward matrix by hand, and say what each one biases toward.
  • Explain why the code compares every selection rule against a random control.
  • Say how best-of-N differs from rejection sampling, and why it still has a KL distance.

What it is, and why it is last

In Lecture 2: 1:01 · 1:33 · 43:39 · 44:10 · 44:43 · 45:14

Rejection sampling is the simplest thing you can do with a reward model for downstream training, popular "especially in labs and people building serious models more so than academic work". 14 He said the same in 2024: "the simplest Training Method". 15 The slides call it "filtered supervised learning": no policy gradients and no online RL, 16 just offline data curation, which can be done in batches. 17 18 Lecture 2 groups it with Chapters 4 and 5, because SFT plus a reward model is all it needs. 19 Together they make "the simplest complete path" to a preference-tuned model, with RS as the optimizer that RL will replace. 20

The name comes from computational statistics. You draw samples from a distribution that is easy to sample from, and accept or reject each one with a check. Here the easy distribution is the current model, the check is a reward model, and the target is high-quality completions. 4 Lambert admits he does not really understand the underlying term. 21

WebGPT, Anthropic's Helpful and Harmless agent, OpenAI's process reward model paper and Llama 2 Chat all used it. 12 RAFT and RSO formalized it. 22 Llama 3 used it too, alongside DPO, over several rounds. 23 It can sit after SFT, after RL or after RLVR, which makes it hard to place. Together with how little is written about it, that is why the chapter comes last among the optimization methods. 24 DeepSeek R1, for example, applies it to an RL checkpoint to build about 800K SFT samples. 25

Lambert includes it because it is widely used and under-researched. 26 It has never been replicated as a minimal script with a dataset you could download. 27 He says much the same in his course introduction ("zero documentation on the web") 28 and in Lecture 1. 29

The process, step by step

In Lecture 2: 45:44 · 46:14

  1. Choose prompts and a reward model. The simplest choice is to reuse every SFT prompt, though that "can cause some overfitting". You need a trained reward model first. 30
  2. Generate. Sample completions from the model you want to improve, choosing temperature, top-p, length and how many per prompt. 33
  3. Score and select. Rank every completion with the reward model, perhaps deduplicating to one per prompt. Many such choices "come down to empirical ablation studies". 34
  4. Fine-tune. Run SFT on the selected completions, starting from the same checkpoint. 31 35

The reward matrix

With $M$ prompts, which usually come from the instruction training set, 36 and $N$ completions for each, you get an $M\times N$ matrix of completions $Y$. Scoring each one gives a matching matrix of rewards: 37

$$R = \begin{bmatrix} r_{1,1} & \cdots & r_{1,N} \\ \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ r_{M,1} & \cdots & r_{M,N}\end{bmatrix}, \qquad r_{i,j} = \mathcal{R}(y_{i,j}\mid x_i)$$
RuleDefinitionWhat it keeps
Top per prompt$S(R) = [\arg\max_j r_{1,j}, \ldots, \arg\max_j r_{M,j}]$ 5One completion per prompt, so every prompt is represented. 38
Top $K$ overallFlatten $R$ to $R_{flat}$ of length $MN$, then $S_K = \text{argsort}(R_{flat})[-K{:}]$ 6The $K$ best pairs anywhere. Map flat index $k$ back with $i = \lfloor k/N\rfloor + 1$, $j = (k \bmod N) + 1$. 39

In the chapter's example, per prompt gives $S(R) = [1, 2, 1, 3, 4]$, 40 and the top 5 overall are flat indices $[8, 5, 14, 0, 11]$. 41 Top overall gives prompt 3 two completions and prompt 5 none. It "can bias toward easy prompts". 42 Lambert calls per prompt "the most careful method", because it keeps your prompt distribution. 43 He is unsure whether choices like this "make or break doing this at scale". 44

From Lambert's own attempts: be careful with sampling settings, get enough diversity, and spend enough inference to tell which completion is best. 54 Projects like Olmo and Tülu spend "millions of dollars in compute" on generation. 55 The SFT step showed no clear differences from standard SFT, though different hyperparameters might help. 56

One throughput tip: sort completions by length before batch reward-model inference, to cut padding. 57 Good inference tools do this automatically, but "a lot of reward model inference tools aren't that great". 58

The selection code is short. Here are the two chapter rules and their random controls:

def select_top_per_prompt(records: list[Record]) -> list[TrainingPair]:
    """Argmax completion per prompt."""
    pairs: list[TrainingPair] = []
    for rec in records:
        if not rec["completions"]:
            continue
        best_idx = max(range(len(rec["rewards"])), key=lambda j: rec["rewards"][j])
        pairs.append((rec["question"], rec["completions"][best_idx]))
    return pairs


def select_top_k_overall(records: list[Record], k: int) -> list[TrainingPair]:
    """Top-k completions across the full M x N reward matrix."""
    flat = [
        (float(reward), rec["question"], completion)
        for rec in records
        for completion, reward in zip(rec["completions"], rec["rewards"], strict=True)
    ]
    flat.sort(key=lambda item: item[0], reverse=True)
    return [(q, c) for _, q, c in flat[:k]]


def select_random_per_prompt(records: list[Record], seed: int) -> list[TrainingPair]:
    """Random baseline for top_per_prompt: pick one completion per prompt uniformly."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    pairs: list[TrainingPair] = []
    for rec in records:
        if not rec["completions"]:
            continue
        pick = rng.randrange(len(rec["completions"]))
        pairs.append((rec["question"], rec["completions"][pick]))
    return pairs


def select_random_k_overall(records: list[Record], k: int, seed: int) -> list[TrainingPair]:
    """Random baseline for top_k_overall: sample k pairs from the flat M x N pool."""
    rng = random.Random(seed)
    flat = [(rec["question"], c) for rec in records for c in rec["completions"]]
    return rng.sample(flat, min(k, len(flat)))

Related: best-of-N sampling

In Lecture 2: 47:47 · 48:17

Best-of-N (BoN) generates and scores the same way, but does not fine-tune. It picks the best completion at inference time, and similar techniques power "Pro" tiers of chat products. 10 59 Lambert calls it "essentially an inference trick". 60 It is "a simple way to do inference time scaling", and a standard baseline for PPO, as in the scaling laws for over-optimization. 61 That over-optimization paper compares BoN with RL as two ways to optimize against a reward model. 62 Unlike RL, it changes no parameters. 63

Because BoN changes which outputs you see, it still has a KL distance from the original policy, and comparisons with PPO stay meaningful. 11 That KL is much smaller than what online RL spends. 64 With one prompt, top per prompt and top $K$ with $K=1$ are the same thing. 65 The slides add that the scorer can be a verifier or an LLM judge instead of a reward model. 59

BoN is also a quick test of a reward model. In 2024 Lambert's team saw "a five to eight point bump, five to 10 point bump" from BoN with their reward models, even though that signal did not necessarily carry over to downstream training. 66 67

Each completion has a true quality drawn from a standard normal. The reward model sees quality plus noise of size $\sigma$. 4,000 simulated prompts, fixed seed. Quality is in standard deviations of the sampler.

Raise the noise and watch the score of the pick pull away from its true quality: the reward model is choosing completions that look good to it. That gap is the seed of over-optimization in Chapter 14. With no noise, $N = 8$ already buys about 1.4 standard deviations of quality. Going from 8 to 64 adds less than going from 1 to 8.

Run it

The book's code runs the whole pipeline on GSM8K: generate, score, select, fine-tune, evaluate. Its four configs come as matched pairs, so you can ask whether the reward model helps at all. 69 Q&A 2 recommends it as a starter project. 70 Each reward-selected config has a random control with the same number and shape of training pairs; "the only difference is whether selection uses the reward or a coin flip". 45 For top-K overall, $K$ is 1,000, the same budget as one per prompt over 1,000 prompts. 71 48 I did not run it.

  1. Check the worked example: uv run python -m rejection_sampling.selection asserts the chapter's selections. 72
  2. Build the rollout cache once with rejection_sampling.preprocess. Later configs reuse it while generation and scoring stay the same. 73
  3. Train the four configs and compare in pairs. If a reward-selected run does not beat its random control, the reward model or completions give no useful signal on that slice. 74
  4. Vary N, temperature, top-p and K, 68 or try a smaller policy to see whether RS rescues weak generations or just picks among good ones. 75

The reference results: on the 1k-train, 200-test slice, top K overall beat its random control, while top per prompt and its random control "were effectively tied". The README warns to treat those gaps as noise. 76 The diagnostics script explains one reason. Per-prompt selection only matters on prompts with both correct and incorrect completions. When most prompts are all right or all wrong, its signal shrinks "even if the RM is well-calibrated". 77

On your Mac: the trainer resolves to CUDA or CPU only. 78 agent note Generating 8,000 completions with a 1.7B model and scoring them with a 7B reward model on CPU would be very slow, and the 7B model alone needs about 15 GB in 16-bit. That is my estimate, and I have not tested it.

Smaller traps

  • Stage numbers. The chapter counts 0 to 3, 30 the slides 1 to 4. 32
  • Two index conventions. $S(R)$ is 1-indexed, $[1, 2, 1, 3, 4]$, 40 while the flat indices are 0-indexed, $[8, 5, 14, 0, 11]$. 41 The mapping formula adds 1 to convert. 39
  • Order. argsort(...)[-K:] returns indices in ascending order of reward, 6 but the chapter lists them best first. 41
  • Captions mishear. "best event sampling" is best-of-N, 63 "projection sampling" is rejection sampling, 79 "two3" is Tülu 3 and "SF" is SFT. 80 Quotes keep these errors.

Check yourself

What separates rejection sampling from best-of-N?

Only the last step. Rejection sampling fine-tunes on the selected completions. Best-of-N returns the best one at inference and leaves the model unchanged. 10

In the chapter's matrix, which prompt gets nothing under top 5 overall, and why might that matter?

Prompt 5, whose best reward is only 0.6. Top overall favors prompts with high rewards, which can mean easy prompts, so the training data drifts away from the original prompt mix. 42 43

Why compare against a random selection of the same size?

Because SFT on the model's own completions may help by itself. The random control isolates whether choosing by reward adds anything. 45 74

Your policy solves 95% of prompts every time. Why might top per prompt barely beat random?

On a prompt where every completion is correct, any pick is as good as the best. Selection only matters on prompts with mixed results. 77

Why use temperature above zero, and many completions?

Selection needs diversity to choose from, and enough samples to find a clearly better one. Too few makes training biased or noisy. 7 8 54

Best-of-N never changes the weights. How can it have a KL distance?

The distribution of what you receive changes, because you only see the winner of $N$ samples. That distribution can be compared with the original policy, 11 and its KL is far smaller than online RL's. 64

Agent notes

agent note Ties at the cut. Not Lambert. In the chapter's matrix the 4th and 5th best rewards are both 0.7, at flat indices 0 and 11. 41 With $K = 4$, the code's stable descending sort keeps index 0. 46 np.argsort(R)[-4:], as in the chapter's snippet, 47 kept index 0 in my run with numpy's default sort, but index 11 with kind="stable". With $K = 5$ all three agree. Real rewards are rarely exactly tied, but rounded or binary scores often are.

agent note The KL of best-of-N. My derivation, not Lambert's. If every completion has a distinct reward, the winner of $N$ samples has probability $N F^{N-1}$ times its original density, where $F$ is the share of samples with lower reward. The KL from the original policy is then $\log N - \frac{N-1}{N}$: 0 at $N=1$, 1.20 nats at $N=8$, 3.17 at $N=64$. Ties can only lower it. It grows like $\log N$, which fits the chapter's claim that BoN's KL is far smaller than RL's. 64

agent note What the simulation assumes. Not Lambert. Quality is normal, the reward model's error is normal and independent of quality, and the pick is the highest score. Real reward models have systematic biases, such as for length, which this does not model. It shows the direction of the effect, not its size.
