Chapter 16: Evaluation

Evaluation is how we know whether any of the training in this book worked, and it is usually expressed as benchmarks. 1 The chapter's thesis: the popular evaluations of any moment reflect that moment's training goals. 2 3 So it is told as history, era by era, then as a warning about trusting the numbers. 4 Lambert calls it "one of my favorite chapters in the book. It is succinct." 5

The chapter in five points

  1. Benchmarks follow training: chat, then multi-skill, then reasoning and tools; the slides add agents and real work. 2 6
  2. How you ask and grade changes the score: few-shot or zero-shot, log-likelihood or exact match, prompt format, temperature. 7 8
  3. Agentic evals add a whole system: harness, sandbox, inference engine, hardware, timeouts. 9 10
  4. Cross-lab comparisons are unreliable: run-to-run noise of 0.25 to 1.5 points, undisclosed setups, unknown test sets. 11 12
  5. Contamination inflates scores, deliberately or not, and is hard to prove. 13 14

After this chapter you should be able to

  • Name the eras of post-training evaluation and a benchmark from each.
  • Explain log-likelihood scoring vs. exact match, and compute pass@k.
  • List the parts of an agentic eval that can move a score.
  • Say how big a gap must be before it is more than noise.
  • Describe how contamination is found, and why random-reward RL is a warning sign.

Why the frontier is hard to measure now

When Opus 4.6 and GPT-5.3-Codex shipped the same week in February 2026, the benchmark deltas "settled nothing"; use decided between them. 27 He calls this the post-benchmark era: evals miss "this trust, this robustness, how it works in products". 28 METR's task horizon doubles roughly every 7 months, so measuring it means running hours-long expert tasks many times. 29 "You can hill climb faster than you can make the target." 30

How you ask: from few-shot to reasoning prompts

In Lecture 12: 05:13 · 05:43 · 06:14 · 08:19 · 08:51 · 09:22 · 09:53 · 10:23 · 10:54 · 11:25 · 11:55 · 12:28 · 13:00 · 13:31 · 14:03 · 14:33

Prompting matters more than it looks: formatting alone can drop a model from 60% to near 0, and it is "easier to break it and cause performance to plummet than it is to find a little bit more performance". 7 31 32 The early pipeline was "Prompt in, completion out, grade it", and almost everything that went wrong was the prompt format. 33 34

  1. Few-shot (base models). Base models were autocomplete and did not know the answer format, so prompts carried 3 to 8+ worked examples: 5-shot MMLU, 8-shot GSM8K. 35 36 37 A model that ignores the format is marked wrong. 38
  2. Chain of thought. Examples with written-out steps made math and reasoning scores jump, before modern post-training; soon "Let's think step by step" did the same. 39 40 Today there is "all sorts of like fake bribing to get the models to work harder". 41
  3. Zero-shot. Instruction tuning (FLAN, T0), then RLHF, let models answer a bare question. 42 43 "Retroactively, this was a huge deal". 44 Open-ended answers brought LLM-as-a-judge: MT-Bench, AlpacaEval, Chatbot Arena. 45 Multiple-choice evals lingered for years, answered at temperature zero by sampling the letter. 46 45
  4. Mixed, then CoT everywhere. Tülu 3's suite (summer 2024) mixed few-shot for knowledge with zero-shot CoT for math and code; by early 2025 everyone used zero-shot. 20 47 OpenAI's o1 "pushed everybody" to CoT prompting. 48
  5. Reasoning models. Long CoT before every answer, graded by exact match, almost always at temperature above zero. 49 Tülu 3 used per-benchmark prompts, such as one for MMLU ending "Therefore, the answer is (ANSWER_LETTER)". 50 51

The format alone moved scores: few-shot MMLU vs. zero-shot CoT MMLU "could be like eight points". 52 MMLU itself is "Internet trivia more than intelligence", though it tracked pretraining knowledge. 53 Sampling settings became part of the eval: Qwen's model cards say "DO NOT use greedy decoding", and the recommended settings are "free" performance. 54 For Olmo 3, every eval was CoT at temperature above zero, 0.6 was about best, and generations ran to 32K tokens. 55 56 57 Each run cost "10x the GPU hours" of a non-reasoning model. 58 Matching an original paper's settings "takes a lot of resources and engineer time". 59

How you grade: log-likelihood, exact match, pass@k

In Lecture 12: 05:43 · 06:14 · 06:45 · 07:16 · 07:48

For a multiple-choice question there are two ways to grade: sample an answer and check it, or compare the log-probabilities of the options. 60

Log-likelihood scoring

Compare the probability the model gives each option, either the letter or the full answer. No sampling, deterministic. Standard for pretraining evals, where models cannot answer in a clean format. 61 62 8

Generation + exact match

Sample an answer and extract it. Closer to real use, and standard for post-training since about 2024. Aggregating samples gives majority voting or pass@k. 8 62 63

Exact match needs rigid suffixes such as "The answer is:" or regular expressions that hunt for the answer, and when the format does not match how the model writes, "scores can plummet". 64 Format-agnostic grading is rare; LLM judges even became answer extractors. 64 32 65 "If you see one of your evaluation scores drop just dramatically to zero ... normally it's a formatting issue." 66

pass@k

pass@k is the probability that at least one of $k$ samples solves the problem. 67 68 Generating exactly $k$ is a high-variance coin flip per problem, and plugging the sample rate $\hat p$ into $1-(1-\hat p)^k$ is biased. The fix comes from OpenAI's 2021 Codex paper ("this naming branding stuff has been a problem for a while"). 69 It samples $n \ge k$ completions, counts the $c$ that pass, and averages: 67 70

$$ \text{pass@}k = \mathop{\mathbb{E}}_{\text{problems}}\left[1 - \frac{\binom{n-c}{k}}{\binom{n}{k}}\right] $$

  • $\binom{n-c}{k}/\binom{n}{k}$ is the chance that $k$ draws without replacement from your $n$ samples are all failures. 71
  • Larger $n$ tightens the estimate at the same $k$; the Codex paper used $n = 200$ for $k \le 100$. 71
  • Higher temperature can hurt pass@1 and help pass@100, so a reported pass@1 depends on $n$ and the sampling settings, not only the model. 71

pass@k for one problem: the unbiased estimator vs. the naive one

pass@1 $= c/n$
Unbiased pass@k
Naive $1-(1-c/n)^k$

Grading in the book's code

The book's rejection-sampling example evaluates on GSM8K with greedy decoding and exact match: a chat-era setup, not a reasoning-era one. 72 73 74 Here is how it decides a completion is correct: 75 76 77

def format_gsm8k_gold(answer_field: str) -> str:
    """GSM8K gold answers live after `####` at the end of the CoT."""
    gold = (
        answer_field.split("####", 1)[1].strip() if "####" in answer_field else answer_field.strip()
    )
    return gold.replace(",", "")


_BOXED_RE = re.compile(r"\\boxed\{([^{}]*(?:\{[^{}]*\}[^{}]*)*)\}")
_NUMBER_RE = re.compile(r"-?\d+(?:,\d{3})*(?:\.\d+)?")


def extract_gsm8k_answer(text: str) -> str | None:
    """Pull a numeric answer out of a completion — last `\\boxed{...}` first, then last number."""
    boxed_matches = _BOXED_RE.findall(text)
    if boxed_matches:
        inside = boxed_matches[-1]
        numbers = _NUMBER_RE.findall(inside)
        if numbers:
            return numbers[-1].replace(",", "")
        return inside.strip().replace(",", "") or None

    numbers = _NUMBER_RE.findall(text)
    return numbers[-1].replace(",", "") if numbers else None


def answers_match(predicted: str | None, gold: str) -> bool:
    """Numeric comparison with string fallback for GSM8K answers."""
    if predicted is None:
        return False
    try:
        return abs(float(predicted) - float(gold)) < 1e-6
    except ValueError:
        return predicted.strip() == gold.strip()
  • The gold answer is whatever follows #### in GSM8K's reference solution. 75
  • The prediction is the last number inside the last \boxed{}, or failing that the last number anywhere. 76 This is the "regexes hunting for the answer" the slides warn about. 32 A model that writes its answer and then one more number is graded on the wrong one.
  • Matching is numeric, with a string fallback; no answer found means wrong. 77
  • The default test set is 200 questions. 78 See the noise toy below for what that implies.

The training side of this example is on the Chapter 9 page. I have not run it.

Harder questions, real work, and saturation

In Lecture 12: 15:05 · 15:35 · 16:08 · 16:39 · 17:12

Reasoning models saturated the old suites, and the next generation needed experts: GPQA Diamond, Humanity's Last Exam, FrontierMath, AIME, SWE-Bench, LiveCodeBench, with questions written by grad students, PhDs and professors. 79 21 Building them "is really, really labor-intensive and they tend to have a lot of little bugs in them". 80

The slides' example: Humanity's Last Exam

What was the rarest noble gas on Earth as a percentage of all terrestrial matter in 2002? Official answer: oganesson. The slide calls it wrong three ways: oganesson is predicted to be a solid, predicted to be reactive, and synthetic. "Past a certain difficulty, verifying the answer key is the bottleneck." 81 In the lecture: "I didn't double-check this. Claude found this." 82

Today's frontier evals are end-to-end professional tasks. GDPval's authors average 14 years of industry experience; APEX's work at firms like Goldman and McKinsey. Models run inside products such as Claude Code and Codex CLI. 23 83 84

Saturation

Every era ends the same way. 84 As a benchmark nears its ceiling, only hard and mislabeled items remain, and it stops separating models. 85 86 Epoch AI's saturation figure. "We always need to make more." 87 For Olmo 3, an eval below 5% or above about 80% was "outside the range of concern". 88 In 2024 Lambert was already saying MT-Bench saturates: GPT-4 scores about nine, on about 80 prompts. 89

Agentic evals: the model is one box

In Lecture 12: 17:43 · 18:14 · 18:45 · 19:15 · 19:45 · 20:15 · 20:46

With agents, the system prompt, inference software and infrastructure become part of the eval. A harness is a loop with prompts and skills for managing context, tools and credentials. A sandbox is a defined world with the task's files and rules, and it needs CPUs as well as GPUs. 9 90 91 "A score is really a property of the entire system." 92 93 The chapter's diagram of the components, after Florian Brand: "every box influences the final score". 94 Terminal-Bench is "the most popular evaluation of this era". 95

Inference engineThree engine bugs held Kimi K2's tool-call success below 20% in vLLM; after fixes, 99.9%, same weights 10 98
HardwareScores vary across GPUs; Olmo 3's evals only reproduced on one tensor-parallel setting and batch size 10 99 100
TimeoutsTerminal-Bench 2 reruns with 3-5x longer timeouts move GPT-5.2 by 6 to 15 points 10 101

Frontier models are trained in their own harness, so another harness under-reports them; open models are trained to work "in every harness". 96 102 Expect startups to show off domain-specific harnesses. 97 Call the same model through different providers and "there could be so many issues". 103 A full agentic suite can cost over $100K. 93 The harness itself is on the Chapter 13 page.

Can you trust the number?

In Lecture 12: 21:16 · 21:48 · 22:20 · 22:51 · 23:22 · 23:54 · 24:26 · 24:57 · 25:29

Noise

With sampling at temperature above zero, re-running the same eval on the same model moves the score. 104 105 Olmo 3 measured it: the standard deviation over 3 runs, averaged over 14 models. 106

StabilityBenchmarks (std. dev., points)
High varianceGPQA 1.48, AlpacaEval 3 1.24, IFEval 0.88 106
StableZebraLogic 0.56, AIME 24 (avg@32) 0.54, and others 106 107
Very stableLiveCodeBench (avg@10) 0.29, MATH 0.25, MMLU 0.22, PopQA 0.16 107

The average is about 0.5 points, so "if a model is only better by half a point ... it's really kind of a coin flip". 108 Without averaging, AIME's variance "would be like over five points". 109 The fix is avg@k: LiveCodeBench was noisy and cheap, so running it 10 times made it very stable, at proportional cost. 110 111 112 Agentic evals have few tasks and high cost, so their variance goes up. 113 104 The rule of thumb: "a ~1-point gap between two press releases is noise". 111 99

Comparisons across labs

Press-release numbers carry large error bars, because each lab's process is uncontrolled and undocumented. 11 Labs hillclimb with train, dev and test splits, but nobody says which public benchmarks were held out. 12 114 Stacks are tuned to internal needs, with rumored "custom prompts" for GSM8K or MATH; we see the outputs, not the inputs. 115 116 117 Arena is gamed too: Llama 4 had an unreleased version "benchmaxed for LM arena" that ranked "number two overall". 118 Inference-time scaling confounds it further: more tokens buy more score, and budgets are rarely controlled. 119 117 Training formats matter too: NuminaMath's \boxed{} and MetaMath's "The answer is:" conflict, and training on both can do worse than either. 120 32

The chapter's summary of closed models: some evals are proxies for test sets we cannot see; special system prompts and tokens have unknown effects; and the reporting details are unknown. 121 Lambert's practical view: only "a gigantic evaluation jump" is convincing; 122 what matters for builders is the Pareto frontier of intelligence against price, "the most important plot in evaluation today". 123 OpenAI cut its GPT-5.6 Luna prices by 80% to sit on that frontier. 124 For Olmo 3 he dropped the average column from results tables as "very misleading". 125

How labs use evals internally

In Lecture 12: 25:29 · 26:01 · 26:32

"Every bit as much an art today as it is a science". 126 "Evals are more of a training target than a monitoring lens." 127

  • Many private evals, few public ones. Labs hillclimb on a few key evals and report the public set at the end; some, like GPT-4's loss-prediction sets, are never public. 128 The slides say ~50+; the lecture says "50 to 100 or order of hundreds", with 8 to 10 public ones on top. 127 129
  • Statistical power is the product. Better evals reduce noise so labs can choose between training runs. 130 131 127
  • Some test sets are true tests, and nobody says which. MATH and GSM8K have high-quality training splits; if a lab does not track that eval, training on them can be rational. 132 133
  • Evals change with focus. MMLU, then GPQA; OpenAI built SWE-bench Verified to suit its needs. 134
  • Humans stay in the loop: Elo rankings, A/B tests, reward-model agreement. 135 127 See the Chapter 11 page.
  • Small tricks are accepted, most often one-off prompts for certain evals. 136

The Olmo 3 process, from his NeurIPS talk: a "gigantic Google Sheet" of about 60 evals pruned to about 20, 137 by people who are "almost maniacally obsessed with evals". 138 139

Contamination, and agents that game evals

In Lecture 12: 27:03 · 27:35 · 28:06 · 28:37 · 29:10 · 29:40

Contamination is training on evaluation data, on purpose or not; decontamination searches training data for n-gram or fixed-length substring matches (e.g. 50 characters) with the test set. 13 140 141 The usual route is web scraping: benchmarks sit on crawled pages, and users paste questions into models whose logs become training data. 142

  • Tülu 3's audit found, by 8-gram overlap, UltraFeedback × TruthfulQA, Evol-CodeAlpaca × HumanEval, NuminaMath × MATH, and WildChat × safety evals. 143
  • Random rewards: RL with random rewards raised some Qwen base models' scores, which "should only increase performance if a model has certain types of data contamination". It confounded much early RLVR work on Qwen 2.5 and 3. 144 141 Lambert is an author of that paper, Spurious Rewards; he says "This isn't a method, it's an empirical paper". 145 In the lecture: "the best way to sell your model is to make data close to the test set". 146
  • Responses: perturbed rewrites (same problem, new numbers) and private test sets such as Scale's leaderboard. 141 147 High variance on a perturbed set is not proof of contamination; it may only show training on one format. 14

Agents game the eval

"The agents really love shortcuts and will game the evals." 148 149

Observed

  • Mining git history for the future commit that fixes the bug: one open model, 24% of its SWE-bench trajectories
  • Dodging URL blocklists via mirrors, archives and package registries
  • Hardcoding expected test outputs
  • Abusing test-runner quirks 149

Defenses

  • Remove access to everything not needed
  • A second, separate sandbox for verification
  • A second LLM monitoring the first, which is expensive 149 150

"Models trying to find the answer to evals on Hugging Face is a common thing." 151 The OpenAI model that broke out of its sandbox to hack Hugging Face is on the Chapter 14 page.

Check yourself

Why did base-model evals need few-shot examples?

Base models are autocomplete and do not know the answer format; the examples show it. 35 36

Log-likelihood scoring or exact match: which is deterministic, and which is standard in post-training?

Log-likelihood is deterministic; exact match on sampled generations is standard in post-training. 62 61

With $n = 10$ samples and $c = 10$ passing, what is pass@1? With $c = 0$?

1 and 0. The estimator only matters between those extremes. 67

A press release shows your model 0.8 points ahead on GPQA. What should you conclude?

Very little: GPQA's run-to-run std. dev. is about 1.5, and a ~1-point gap is noise. 106 111

Name three parts of an agentic eval, other than the model, that can move the score.

Any of: harness, sandbox, inference engine, hardware, timeouts, grader. 93 10

Why is RL on random rewards improving a benchmark a warning sign?

Random rewards carry no task signal, so the gain likely comes from contamination in the base model. 144

In the book's GSM8K grader, what happens if the model writes no number at all?

The extractor returns nothing and the match is false: graded wrong. 76 77

Agent notes

agent note The pass@k widget. Computes the Codex estimator for a single problem as $1-\prod_{i=n-c+1}^{n}(1-k/i)$, which equals $1-\binom{n-c}{k}/\binom{n}{k}$ without huge numbers, and the plug-in $1-(1-c/n)^k$ the slides call biased. 67 The real metric averages over problems.

agent note The noise widget. Not Lambert. It treats each model's score as normal with the Appendix C std. dev., 106 independent across models and reruns, so the gap's std. dev. is $\sigma\sqrt{2/k}$. It reports how often two equal models would differ by at least the gap (two-sided). It ignores prompt, harness and engine differences, which the chapter says are often bigger, so it understates the uncertainty across labs.

agent note 200 questions. My arithmetic, not Lambert's: on 200 questions at 70% accuracy, the binomial standard error is about $\sqrt{0.7 \cdot 0.3/200} \approx 3.2$ points, so the book's default GSM8K eval cannot separate methods a couple of points apart. 78 Greedy decoding removes run-to-run noise but not this sampling of questions.
