Chapter 11: Preference Data

We cannot write down a loss for human preferences, so preference data is the proxy signal RLHF tunes against. 1 Lambert names two challenges: collecting it is costly and operationally complex, and it needs to be "on-policy", generated by the model being trained. 2 Chapter 10 asked what a preference is. This chapter is about the mechanics: interfaces, formats, contracts and biases. Lambert calls collecting it well "one of the most opaque pieces of the pipeline". 3

The chapter in five points

  1. Judging beats generating. It is easier to tell a good answer from a bad one than to write a good one, which is why this data works. 4
  2. On-policy matters. Preferences should be collected on completions from your own family of models. 5
  3. Rankings, binarized. Data is collected as ratings or Likert-scale rankings, then usually reduced to chosen and rejected. 6 7
  4. Buying it is hard. Vendors are supply-limited, contracts run in weekly batches, and much of the spend is "wasted". 8 9 10
  5. Biases ride along. Prefix bias, sycophancy, verbosity and formatting pass from the data into the model. 11

After this chapter you should be able to

  • Explain what "on-policy" means for preference data, and why it is softer than in RL.
  • Compare ratings and rankings, and 5-point and 8-point Likert scales, and say what binarizing loses.
  • Build a structured preference pair from a checkable constraint.
  • Describe a human-data contract from vendor to delivery, and where it goes wrong.
  • Name the common biases in preference data.

Why we need preference data

In Lecture 8: 20:16 · 20:46

One reward function cannot capture complex human values, as Chapter 10 argued. Collecting comparisons to train reward models is one of RLHF's original ideas. It works because it is far easier, for people and for AI supervisors, to tell a good answer from a bad one than to write the good one. 4 12 The data is so rich "that it is difficult to replace this style of optimization at all". 13 Human preference data mattered most when RLHF was the final step, and "is still definitely used to understand and improve the models today". 14

Collecting preference data

In Lecture 8: 21:17 · 21:49 · 30:06

Doing it well means iterative training, hundreds of thousands or millions of dollars, detailed instructions and data vendors in between. 3 As of 2026 no open model has released fully open human preference data together with how it was collected. The closest are NVIDIA's HelpSteer datasets. 15 16 Lambert sees this as a part of the literature that got "left behind". 17 Many new teams skip human data entirely, for AI feedback or off-the-shelf reward models. 18

On-policy, in a softer sense

The best preference data is assumed to come from the previous checkpoints of your own training. 19 In RL, on-policy means data from the latest policy. For preference data it means data from "the current family of models", and research shows this beats pooled completions from popular models. 5 Little documents this, and the balance of human and AI preference data in frontier models is unknown. 20 At Hugging Face, Lambert's small team found on-policy collection "really hard", so they bought SFT data, which has no such constraint. 21 22

One subtlety: the chosen answer is not necessarily correct, only better than the alternatives shown. Models can learn even when every completion is right, or every one wrong. 23

Rankings vs. ratings

In Lecture 8: 23:54 · 24:24 · 24:55

The biggest design choice: ratings score one completion on its own, and rankings compare completions. Training usually uses rankings, with ratings kept as metadata. 6 38 39 A 1-to-5 rating rubric is one simple option. 40 UltraFeedback turns ratings into pairs: the top-rated completion against a random lower one. 41

Most rankings use a Likert scale. It too is a single integer, so the difference from a rating is the structure, not the number. 42

Early Anthropic work used 8 points, which forces a direction. 44 45 Either way, the information is "still reduced to a binary signal" for the reward model. 7 Lambert says Likert strength "was at times it was used for training but it never really stuck". 46

Multi-turn data

Usually only the final turn is labeled. When every turn is labeled, the conversation continues with the chosen answer, and a long conversation can be unrolled into several training prompts, carefully, to avoid bias. 48 Earlier turns are masked from the loss, as in SFT. 49 An open question, after John Schulman: should the person who writes the prompt also label the responses, to avoid sycophancy? If not, multi-turn is hard in practice, because conversations must continue in real time. 50

Structured preference data

In Lecture 8: 25:25 · 25:56 · 26:29 · 27:01

Some domains label themselves. In math, a correct solution beats an incorrect one. 51 52 For instruction following, as in IFEval, you sample the same model twice, with and without a constraint like "Start each sentence with the letter g", and prefer the one that obeys it. 53 The check is code, "so the label is free". 54 The prompt in the pair must include the constraint. 55 Pairs like this have brought "meaningful performance improvements" on instruction following and math, 55 and the slides say they "can beat quality-judged preferences" in narrow domains. 54

Alternatives to pairs

Single thumbs-up or thumbs-down labels with KTO, token-level feedback, and written critiques all give richer signal at a higher collection cost. They have "not been pursued in as much detail". 60 61 In Lambert's telling, KTO is "pretty effective in industry", 62 63 while the richer kinds "faded away", like process reward models, as too hard to collect. 64

Sourcing and contracts

In Lecture 8: 27:32 · 28:02 · 28:33 · 29:05 · 30:36

The chapter describes buying human preference data when the field is moving fast, and expects it to become far more automated. 65 The steps:

  1. Find a vendor. It is "a who-you-know game": vendors are supply-limited and favor big budgets and famous brands, with discounts on early batches "to get training teams hooked". 8 66 67
  2. Sign, carefully. Some vendors delivered only under threat of legal action. Default terms often forbid open-sourcing the data. 68
  3. Write the instructions. Detailed documents of corner cases and priorities. The public example is OpenAI's InstructGPT instructions, 69 once deleted and recovered by Lambert. 70 71
  4. Book the time. Math and code labelers must be scheduled weeks out; vendors plan their workforce like labs plan compute. 72
  5. Collect in weekly batches. A typical contract runs 6 weeks, about $500K for a smaller one. Early weeks calibrate, and more early data gets thrown out. 9 73 You ramp up as the data aligns with what you want. 74
  6. Improve the model by week 4 or 5, as with Llama 2's 14 stages, but "it doesn't always go well". 75

The chapter's figure shows the batch cycle. Your training pipeline must be ready before the data arrives, or the data goes stale. 76 Millions are probably "wasted", and few organizations can make full use of human data. 10 Lambert's warning: "a lot of times people buy data and nothing happens", so don't buy data until your own methods are established. 77 78

His own purchase at Hugging Face was No Robots: 10,000 human-written demonstrations, released openly, which is rare. 79 80 The vendor market has changed. Scale AI once relied on "primarily overseas and low-skill workforces"; 81 now vendors "need high-skill workforce". 82 And preference comparison became a business: Arena reached about $100M in annualized revenue. 83 84

FormattingLists and bold look better; the "markdownification" of chat answers 11 86

Sycophancy reflects human tendencies outside any labeling criteria; verbosity and formatting are easier to detect and mitigate. 11 Lambert blames "lazy labelers" in part. 86 These are what over-optimization amplifies, 87 and mitigating them separates "good and great preference data". 88 It is "an infinite problem": there will always be new biases. 89 Paid and unpaid annotators bring different issues. 90

Open questions

A preference between two texts captures "a broad and diverse function via extremely limited interactions", mostly from professional workforces. 91 The chapter lists four questions: collection context, type of feedback, population demographics, and whether the preferences are expressed in the model at all. 47 The Chapter 10 page covers them with Lambert's lecture comments.

Smaller traps

  • What the InstructGPT instructions cover. The slides call them labeler instructions for ranking, 70 but Lambert is "not sure if it was for preferences or for completions". 71
  • "Sentence" or line. The chapter's constraint says "each sentence", 53 but its example poem is checked most naturally line by line; the widget checks lines. 56
  • Different examples. The chapter uses a goldfish and the letter g; the slides use the ocean and lowercase. 53 57
  • Captions mishear. "liyker" and "lyker" are Likert, "syphy" and "sycopency" are sycophancy, "conan traverski" is Kahneman-Tversky, "neatron" is Nemotron. 37 62 16 Quotes keep these errors.

Check yourself

What does "on-policy" mean for preference data, and how is it softer than in RL?

Data from the current family of models, not strictly the latest policy as in RL. 5

Ratings or rankings: which do teams usually train on?

Rankings, binarized to chosen and rejected; ratings stay as metadata. 6 39

Why use an 8-point Likert scale instead of 5?

An even scale has no tie, so every label has a direction. 44 45

Must the chosen answer be correct?

No. It only has to be better than the alternatives shown; all might be right, or all wrong. 23

You build an IFEval-style pair but leave the constraint out of the prompt. What goes wrong?

The chosen answer obeys a rule the prompt never asked for, so the model learns no link between instruction and behavior. The chapter says the prompt must include the constraint. 55

Your team has budget for human preference data but no settled post-training pipeline. What does Lambert advise?

Wait. Without an established pipeline, bought data often does nothing and goes stale. 77 76

Agent notes

agent note How the Likert widget reduces votes. Not Lambert. Each labeler's point counts as a vote for A, for B, or as a tie. The pair follows the majority of non-tie votes, and is dropped if those are even. Real pipelines may use one label per pair, average scores, or drop low-margin pairs. The chapter says only that the signal is reduced to binary. 7

agent note Ties on a 5-point scale. Not Lambert. A tie cannot become a chosen/rejected pair, so on a 5-point scale tie votes are lost when the data is binarized. The 8-point scale avoids that by forcing a direction, 44 at the cost of turning genuine indifference into noise.
