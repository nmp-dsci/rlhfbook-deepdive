Chapter 4: Instruction Fine-Tuning

Instruction fine-tuning turns a model that continues text into one that answers. 1 The method is ordinary supervised learning, so the chapter spends its time on what matters for RLHF: how the training data is formatted and structured. 2

The chapter in five points

  1. It comes first. Most of the pipelines in the book, from collecting preferences to online RLHF, cannot run without basic instruction following. 3
  2. The chat template is the format. All post-training stages rely on it to mark who is speaking and when to generate. 4 The lecture spends most of its time here because it is the most often neglected part. 5
  3. Same loss, masked prompts. The loss matches pretraining, 6 but prompt tokens are masked, so the model learns responses and not user queries. 7
  4. Gentler settings. Batches are substantially smaller 8 and the learning rate is one to two orders of magnitude lower. 9
  5. The data grew up. About 10K human samples were once state of the art, and large synthetic datasets now work best. 10 Around 1M prompts is the chapter's rule of thumb, 11 though Lambert's sources differ on how far that holds. The prompts should match the tasks you care about. 12

After this chapter you should be able to

  • Render a conversation through a chat template by hand, and say why generation stops.
  • Say which tokens are in the loss under each multi-turn masking strategy.
  • Explain why SFT uses a smaller batch and a lower learning rate than pretraining.
  • Plan an SFT dataset: where prompts and completions come from, and how to mix them.

Why instruction tuning exists

In Lecture 2: 2:35 · 3:39 · 4:42

Ask a base model "What is the capital of France?" and it may continue with more questions. An instruction-tuned model answers "The capital of France is Paris." 1 The chapter says this took off when two lines of work converged: a unified text-to-text framing of tasks, and evidence from scaling and prompting that one model could generalize across tasks. 13 The slides add that SFT took off after ChatGPT, with Alpaca, OpenAssistant and Tulu. 14

Lambert's 2025 talk calls SFT "the base of all of this", the stage where normal chat models still get most of their performance. 15 The slides say to start any post-training by seeing how far IFT can go. 16 Later stages now blur into it: the lecture mentions overlap with mid-training, 17 and Q&A 3 says the two are becoming indistinguishable. 18

Chat templates

In Lecture 2: 5:13 · 7:51 · 8:53 · 10:27

A pretrained model knows only a few special tokens, and it generates until its context runs out or it emits end-of-sequence. 22 The chat template adds structure on top of that. Data is stored as model-agnostic messages, and the template renders them in a tokenizer-specific form, 19 using a Jinja snippet stored in the tokenizer configuration. 23 24

RoleHolds 25
systemInstructions for the agent, not exposed to the user. Only in the first message.
userMessages from the person using the AI.
assistantResponses from the model.

Generation works by ending the prompt with an open assistant tag. The model continues from there until it emits its end token. 26 Templates differ across models, and many also carry tokens for tool use. 27 28

What the lecture adds

  • This is where things break. Jinja is "extremely ugly and hard to read", 29 and a mismatch with the tokenizer can break training. 28 Lambert: "it has caused me so much pain in my life." 5
  • Harmony is an alternative. OpenAI's format splits output into analysis, commentary and final channels, because Jinja cannot cleanly handle tool calls. 30 Lambert thinks it is a bit better, but many people have not switched. 31

Best practices

In Lecture 2: 12:00 · 14:41 · 16:49 · 17:51 · 19:24

The chapter's four principles, with what the talks add:

  • Quality. Completions are what the model learns from. 32 The lecture agrees, then warns that completions are only as good as the prompts, and calls prompts "the fundamental unit of post-training". 33
  • Scale. Around 1M prompts is enough for excellent RLHF. 11 It is not a magic number: you need about 1 to 10K prompts per domain, and mixing domains adds up to a million. 34
  • Distribution. The best prompts resemble your downstream tasks. 12 Excellent completions to the wrong prompts will not give you a good model. 35
  • Forgiveness. Later stages can recover from some noise, so the overall optimization matters more than any one stage. 36

Building a dataset

  1. Mix first. Combine existing datasets for your tasks and measure. 46 Often this just works, and it fails when language conflicts across subdomains. 47
  2. Expand prompts with self-instruct. Start with good prompts, have a strong model modify them, then generate completions. This easily gives 10 times more data. 48
  3. Go human where models fall short. Human data is still needed for novel tasks, 48 which at recording meant knowledge work. 49
  4. Repeat. Expect tens or hundreds of training runs. Developing the process is most of the compute. 46

You can reuse some prompts across stages, though stages often have different goals. 50 39

Implementation details

In Lecture 2: 11:30 · 13:36

The loss is the pretraining cross-entropy, 6 written here over demonstration pairs: 51

$$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{SFT}}(\theta) = - \sum_{(x, y^\star)} \sum_{t=1}^{|y^\star|} \log \pi_\theta \!\left(y^\star_t \mid x, y^\star_{<t}\right)$$
TermMeaning
$(x, y^\star)$A prompt and its target response from the dataset. 51
$y^\star_t$Token $t$ of the target response.
$x,\ y^\star_{<t}$What the model sees: the prompt and the response so far.
$\pi_\theta$The model's probability for the next token. Prompt tokens never appear as targets, which is the masking. 7

Masking, interactively

Multi-turn conversations have two common choices. 52 Switch between them to see which tokens count toward the loss. System and user tokens are usually both masked. 53 The prompt distribution differs from the assistant's, which is why this detail matters. 54

Where masking happens

This code uses final-turn masking. 66 It renders the conversation with and without the last reply. The difference is the reply, and everything before it gets label -100, 20 which the loss ignores. 21

    input_ids, labels = [], []
    for prompt_ids, full_ids in zip(prompt_ids_batch, full_ids_batch, strict=True):
        prompt_length = min(len(prompt_ids), len(full_ids))
        if prompt_length == len(full_ids):
            continue

        input_ids.append(full_ids)
        labels.append([IGNORE_INDEX] * prompt_length + full_ids[prompt_length:])

    return {"input_ids": input_ids, "labels": labels}

Smaller traps

  • Role markers are not always special tokens. The chapter describes special tokens separating messages. 74 In OLMo-2, the role markers are plain text pieces, and <|endoftext|> doubles as start, end and unknown. 75 That tokenizer also ships without a template, so the script borrows one. 76
  • Harmony: Python or Rust? The lecture says "they make it all a Python", 31 and the slide says a Rust-based renderer. 30 The slide's own example is called from Python, so both may hold.
  • The example config is not the guidance. It uses a batch of 32 and $5 \times 10^{-6}$, 56 smaller than the OLMo 2 numbers the chapter quotes. 9
  • Two sweep suggestions. The chapter sweeps 1e-6, 5e-6 and 5e-5; 65 the code README compares 5e-6 and 1e-5. 77
  • Keys are not dates. wei2021finetuned has a different year in its entry. 78
  • Captions mishear. "Valve suites" in Lecture 2 19:24 is evaluation suites, and "super provis" in the 2025 talk 15 is supervised. Quotes keep these errors.

Check yourself

Why does a partly trained model still invent new user questions after answering?

It has not yet internalized the template or the convention of stopping after one answer. 62 It stops only by generating the end token, which is what training teaches. 26

A conversation has three assistant replies. Which are in the loss under each strategy?

Final-turn only: the last one. Mask user turns only: all three. With final-turn, you can unroll the conversation into several samples so each reply gets trained. 52

Why does Olmo 3 use a higher SFT learning rate than OLMo 2?

Partly because sequence packing raises the effective batch, and lower-variance gradients support a higher learning rate. 9

Your completions are excellent but the model is weak on your task. What do you check first?

The prompts. Excellent completions to the wrong prompts will not be enough, 35 and the best prompts resemble your downstream tasks. 12

You want math, code and chat skills. Where does a figure like 1M prompts come from?

You need about 1 to 10K prompts in a domain to start seeing improvement, and mixing chunks for many domains reaches that range. 34

Is it reasonable to ship a model after SFT alone?

Sometimes. Q&A 3 says more models could get by with just SFT than people think, especially small ones, 79 and the Olmo 3 talk calls SFT the foundation, needing mainly high-quality data. 80
