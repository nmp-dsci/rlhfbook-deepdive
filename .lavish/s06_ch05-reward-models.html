Chapter 5: Reward Modeling

A reward model is where RLHF learns what people prefer. It plays the part of the environment in RL, except you train it yourself from human preferences, and it stands in as a proxy for what you actually want. 1 Most of the chapter is about one loss and the three other kinds of model it gets confused with.

The chapter in five points

  1. Bradley-Terry is the default. Each completion gets a score, and the probability one beats another depends only on the gap between their scores. 2
  2. The loss is classification. Push the chosen score above the rejected one with $-\log\sigma$ of the gap. 3 Both common forms are binary cross-entropy. 4
  3. Train on pairs, score anything. Training needs chosen and rejected completions, but at inference you can score any single completion. 5
  4. Three other models are easy to confuse with it. An ORM predicts correctness at every token, a PRM scores reasoning steps, and a value function predicts remaining return. 6 An ORM is not a Bradley-Terry model trained on right and wrong answers. 7
  5. Much is still unsettled. The variants have not settled into a best practice, 8 and the lecture calls training good reward models "a art of industry" the academic literature has barely captured. 9

After this chapter you should be able to

  • Derive the reward model loss from the Bradley-Terry model, including where the expectation goes.
  • Say where each of the four model types reads its score, and what its training labels are.
  • Explain why a Bradley-Terry model on correct and incorrect pairs is not an ORM.
  • Say what is known and what is open about LLM-as-a-judge and reward model benchmarks.

Training a Bradley-Terry reward model

In Lecture 2: 22:30 · 25:07 · 27:42 · 31:16

The Bradley-Terry model gives each item a latent strength. Writing the strength as $e^{r_i}$ lets the scores be unbounded, and the preference probability becomes a sigmoid of the score gap: 2

$$P(i > j) = \frac{p_i}{p_i + p_j} = \frac{e^{r_i}}{e^{r_i} + e^{r_j}} = \sigma(r_i - r_j)$$

The slides call it "a useful approximation, not a law of nature", kept because it worked in early RLHF. 10 For a reward model, the items are two completions to one prompt, and a linear head on a language model produces each score. 11

Fit it by maximum likelihood over the dataset, taking the log before averaging, because $\mathbb{E}[P]$ and $\mathbb{E}[\log P]$ are not the same objective: 12

$$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\log \sigma\!\left( r_\theta(y_c \mid x) - r_\theta(y_r \mid x) \right) = \log\!\left(1 + e^{\,r_\theta(y_r \mid x) - r_\theta(y_c \mid x)}\right)$$
TermMeaning
$x$The prompt. 3
$y_c,\ y_r$The chosen and the rejected completion. 3
$r_\theta(y \mid x)$The reward model's scalar score, read at the last token. 13
$\sigma$The sigmoid. Only the score gap matters, so adding a constant to every score changes nothing. 2
Two formsThe log-sigmoid form follows InstructGPT, and the softplus form follows Anthropic's work. Both are binary cross-entropy. 3 4 14

What the model outputs

  • A probability of being chosen, not a quality grade. The chapter says RLHF reward models output the probability that an input is "of high quality", meaning the chosen answer of a pair. 15 At inference that is the relative likelihood a text is chosen, as one logit. 16
  • The loss is the easy part. The challenge is the data loader and inference pipeline, 17 18 and at its best the signal is "a lot of subtle signals". 19

The ORM's per-token loss averages a binary cross-entropy over the $T$ completion tokens, each trained toward the whole answer's label $r$: 22

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{token}}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{(s,r)\sim \mathcal{D}}\left[\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T} \Big( r\log p_\theta(s_t) + (1-r)\log\big(1-p_\theta(s_t)\big) \Big)\right]$$

The confusions Lambert keeps warning about

  • An ORM is not Bradley-Terry on right and wrong answers. That is still a contrastive, sequence-level model. 7 27 The lecture repeats it "because there was a phase where there's a lot of work misconstruing this". 28 29
  • An ORM is not a value function. Both have per-token heads, but ORM targets are fixed offline labels and value targets come from on-policy rollouts. 26 The lecture's shorthand: the ORM lives in probability space and the value function in reward space. 30
  • "ORM" means different things in different papers. The original GSM8K version also trained on next-token prediction, which did not stick, 31 and the term is now used in several ways. 32
  • ORM labels judge only the final answer. Reasoning errors are not captured, and the process is noisy. 33 One use the lecture suggests is looking for a sudden drop in predicted correctness at a math mistake. 34

Each type tends to fill a niche in a recipe rather than sit at its centre. 35 Outcome models are for reasoning-heavy tasks, and the chapter calls them a relatively niche area. 36 At inference, a preference RM reranks completions for best-of-N or provides the terminal reward for RLHF, while an ORM's per-token scores need aggregating, for example by mean or minimum. 37

Other variants

In Lecture 2: 33:51 · 34:52 · 35:23

  • Margin loss. Llama 2 subtracted a margin from rating differences, such as 5 minus 2, so strong preferences get bigger gaps. 38
  • Balancing comparisons. InstructGPT ranked 4 to 9 completions per prompt and weighted the resulting pairs, because they are correlated and naive shuffling overfits. 39
  • K-wise loss. Starling used a Plackett-Luce ranking loss, which reduces to Bradley-Terry when $K = 2$. 40

None of these became standard, and most work uses the simpler form. 8 The sources disagree on what happened to two of them.

LLM-as-a-judge, and evaluating reward models

In Lecture 2: 42:06 · 43:08

Preference data is expensive, so a large area grew up around prompting a language model to judge two completions, as a human labeler would. 41 Judges are very useful for filtering and supervising data, 42 but on reward model benchmarks they tend to trail trained reward models. A common trick is temperature 0 to reduce variance. 43

The academic study of reward models only established itself in 2024, starting with RewardBench, 44 which Lambert helped start. 45 Even so, the lecture says the benchmarks are not well correlated with how reward models are used in RLHF, 46 and the RewardBench2 work did not get complete answers on what makes a good reward model for training. 47

The forward pass reads each score at the last real token and applies the same loss as above: 53

    def get_reward(self, input_ids: torch.Tensor, attention_mask: torch.Tensor) -> torch.Tensor:
        """Compute scalar reward for a sequence.

        Returns the reward from the last non-padding token position.
        """
        hidden = self.get_hidden_states(input_ids, attention_mask)

        # Get last non-padding position for each sequence
        seq_lengths = attention_mask.sum(dim=1) - 1
        batch_indices = torch.arange(hidden.size(0), device=hidden.device)
        last_hidden = hidden[batch_indices, seq_lengths]

        reward = self.head(last_hidden).squeeze(-1)
        return reward

    def forward(
        self,
        chosen_ids: torch.Tensor,
        chosen_mask: torch.Tensor,
        rejected_ids: torch.Tensor,
        rejected_mask: torch.Tensor,
    ) -> tuple[torch.Tensor, torch.Tensor, torch.Tensor]:
        """Compute Bradley-Terry preference loss.

        Returns:
            loss: -log(sigmoid(r_chosen - r_rejected))
            r_chosen: Rewards for chosen responses
            r_rejected: Rewards for rejected responses
        """
        r_chosen = self.get_reward(chosen_ids, chosen_mask)
        r_rejected = self.get_reward(rejected_ids, rejected_mask)

        # Bradley-Terry loss
        loss = -F.logsigmoid(r_chosen - r_rejected).mean()

        return loss, r_chosen, r_rejected

The chapter's third suggestion is a contribution: a 50 to 200 example held-out evaluation that reports pair ordering without a full training run. 54

Smaller traps

  • The code README undersells the ORM. Its table says the ORM "scores full responses", 68 but the script trains per-token labels, as the chapter describes. 69
  • Two PRM label schemes. The chapter's loss is for a binary-labeled PRM, 25 while the traditional heads predict -1, 0 or 1. 24
  • Inverse RL is a cousin, not a parent. Same problem statement, different implementation and focus, so usually studied separately. 70
  • Captions mishear. "Entropic" in Lecture 2 is Anthropic, 71 and "RRM" is ORM. 72 Quotes keep these errors.

Check yourself

Add 10 to every score your reward model outputs. What happens to the loss?

Nothing. Only score differences matter. 2

Why take the log before averaging over the dataset?

Because maximizing the expected probability is not the same as maximizing the expected log-probability, and the negative log-likelihood is the right objective. 12 This is the fix from Q&A 2. 57

You pair a correct and an incorrect answer to each math problem and train with the Bradley-Terry loss. Is it an ORM?

No. It is still a Bradley-Terry preference model, with correctness as the preference. 7 An ORM takes one completion and a label, and predicts at every token. 27

An ORM and a value head have the same shape. How would you tell them apart from the training code?

Look at the targets. An ORM uses fixed offline labels copied to every token, and a value function uses targets computed from on-policy rollouts that change as the policy changes. 26

Why can an ORM train on data a preference RM cannot use?

It needs only one completion and a correctness label, not a pair for the same prompt, so it does not need two forward passes. 23 20

Your judge model and your trained reward model disagree on a pair. Which does the chapter trust more on benchmarks?

The trained reward model, which tends to score better on reward model evaluations. 43 Lambert also says those benchmarks do not track RLHF usefulness well, 46 so neither answer is final.
