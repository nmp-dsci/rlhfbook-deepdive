Chapter 2: A Tiny History of RLHF

Why does the RLHF recipe look the way it does? Because some of its seminal papers were built for applications far from language models. Lambert tells this history to show how new the methods are, and to warn you that details will keep changing. 1

The story in five moves

  1. 2008: a person scores each action. TAMER learns a reward model from those scores. 2
  2. 2017: a person picks between two behaviours. Christiano et al. train a reward predictor from comparisons, and the agent maximizes its predictions. 3
  3. 2018: reward models become a way to study alignment. 4 The bet is that judging an outcome is easier than producing it. 5
  4. 2019 to 2022: the method moves onto language models. Ziegler et al. come first in the book, 6 though one talk disagrees. InstructGPT makes the three-stage recipe canonical. 7
  5. From ChatGPT on: RLHF gets famous, and the original recipe's pieces get swapped out. OpenAI credits RLHF in the announcement, 8 and by 2026 he says almost all the canonical pieces have moved on, at least in what is known openly. 9

After this chapter you should be able to

  • Say what changed between TAMER, Christiano et al. and InstructGPT.
  • Explain what the method lost when it moved from games and robots to language models.
  • Say why Lambert calls RLHF necessary but not sufficient for ChatGPT.
  • Find where his own tellings of this history differ, and check them.

The assumption the rest rests on

His 2023 talk picks out one assumption from Leike et al. and calls it crucial: valuation of outcomes is easier than producing the correct Behavior. 5 Chapter 11 says the same about preference data today: it is far easier to tell a good answer from a bad one than to generate a good one. 16

What his talks add

  • TAMER's scores were single numbers. A person gave every action a score from zero to one, with no comparisons yet. 17
  • Comparison arrives with Christiano et al. He calls it the first paper with pairwise preference models, 18 and says the Bradley-Terry math used in reward modeling came in through it. 19
  • Why read a paper from before language models? Because it shows that preferences can carry information beyond any one action or word. 20
  • The same people kept going. He describes Leike et al. as a heavily overlapping author group, 21 and later as "a bunch of the core people in RHF". 22

2019 to 2022: RL from human preferences on language models

In Lecture 1: 20:47 · 21:49 · 22:54

Labs scaling up language models adopted the method quickly, and much of this work began between GPT-2 in 2019 and GPT-3 in 2020. 24

Ziegler et al. 2019 is the chapter's earliest paper here. Learning reward models, KL distances and feedback diagrams were all formalized in it, though its tasks and model capabilities differ from today's. 6 The lecture calls it underrated: the terms of art were there, "but it was much less known". 25 In Lecture 10 he adds a detail the chapter skips: the paper did not fix the KL weight, and instead picked a target KL and let a controller chase it. 26

What moving to language models changed. The setup he calls "pretty contrived": no environment, a completion that just stops, and one reward per sequence from a proxy. 23 He calls it "the magic" that RL methods still work once the setup is pruned down like this. 27

Three areas these years opened

  • Over-optimization: RL optimizers can over-fit to models trained on preference data. 38 Chapter 14 returns to it, measuring performance against KL distance. 39
  • Language models as a place to study alignment. 38 Lecture 13 adds that this paper, Askell et al. 2021, discusses the helpful, honest and harmless traits. 40
  • Red teaming: assessing a model's safety. 38 After ChatGPT, he recalls, people were still learning "what is red teaming". 41

Open tools came early and struggled

The chapter only says early open-source tools emerged. 36 Lecture 6 is blunter about 2023: most open efforts at full RLHF pipelines "just kind of crashed and burned", trlX was the most successful, and DPO then made post-training far more accessible. 42 Chapter 1 adds that doubt about RLHF lasted, especially in the open, where groups can't afford data budgets of $100K to $1M. 43

2023 to the present: the ChatGPT era

In Lecture 1: 6:18

The chapter's evidence is one sentence from OpenAI's announcement:

We trained this model using Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback (RLHF), using the same methods as InstructGPT, but with slight differences in the data collection setup. 8

RLHF being linked to ChatGPT is why he and many others got excited. He was at Hugging Face at the time, and that is when he started working on language models. 44 He also points out that OpenAI doesn't say what the changes were. 45

How much credit does RLHF get?

His consistent line is necessary but not sufficient. In 2024 he says you can't build a ChatGPT without something like it, yet "pre-training is still most of the work". 46 He repeats the phrase in 2025, 47 and that year also says it "wasn't the leading thing" people were thinking about, "just a cherry on top". 48 In December 2023 he adds a warning: RLHF is "not even robust to fine tuning". 49

Then the labs used it. The chapter lists Constitutional AI, Llama 2 and 3, Nemotron and Tülu 3. 50 He finds a line in the Llama 2 report "really entertaining": it calls RL "some shadowy field" that proved highly effective. He reads it as a snapshot of July 2023, when it wasn't clear RLHF would take off. 51

A catch in that list. Constitutional AI appears as a use of RLHF, but Chapter 12 explains that a language model produces its pairwise preferences, hence the name RLAIF. 52 Those comparisons come from a model, and the rest of RLHF proceeds as normal.

Today the field is wider than RLHF. He calls it preference fine-tuning (PreFT), and the chapter ends by pointing forward: 53

  • Process rewards for intermediate reasoning steps: Chapter 5.
  • Direct alignment algorithms inspired by DPO: Chapter 8.
  • Learning from execution feedback on code or math, and online reasoning methods inspired by o1: Chapter 7.

The opening warning that details will change has already come true. His slides sketch 2023 to 2026 as simple SFT, then DPO, then RLVR, then agentic training, with RLHF becoming "one tool (that you may not even need)". 54 Presenting his own deck in June 2026, he says that, at least in what is known openly, little human demonstration data goes into SFT, he guesses synthetic data has a much bigger role, and reward models aren't the key RL target any more. 9 As he puts it in Q&A 1, "it became a post-training book". 55

Where his tellings differ

Each side carries its date. A later source isn't automatically a correction, so these stay open unless he settles them.

unresolved What was the first RLHF on language models?

  • Ziegler et al. 2019: the book, 6 the slides, 56 his December 2023 talk 57 and Lecture 10 in July 2026, which hedges with "probably". 58
  • Stiennon et al. 2020: his January 2025 talk says "the first example was really like learning to summarize". 59 Chapter 1's list of early breakthroughs also omits Ziegler. 60

Most sources say Ziegler, and I found nowhere that he takes back the 2025 line.

unresolved What did Christiano et al. run on?

  • Atari: the book 14 and the slides. 56
  • Simulated robots: "a walking robot" in December 2023, 61 "actually for control of simulated robotic agents" in March 2025, 62 and the backflip clips in Lecture 1, which he credits to this paper. 63

See the agent note below.

unresolved How strong was that result?

  • Hedged: the book says "in some domains", under "some clever conditions". 14
  • Unhedged: "even more powerful than the reward signal from the environment" in December 2023, 61 and "it's clear that this human feedback worked better" in April 2026. 64

They also compare different things. The lecture's rival is a reward function a human wrote, 65 while the book and the 2023 talk compare against the environment.

unresolved What made RLHF matter?

  • 2020 summarization: "the first paper where people went oh crap" (December 2023), though he adds it took almost three more years for people to realize. 29
  • InstructGPT and Constitutional AI: "the work that popularized RLHF" (April 2026). 66
  • The ChatGPT post itself: RLHF "really became popular" with that quote (January 2025). 67

unresolved Who came up with the three-step recipe?

  • InstructGPT: "defined" it (April 2026) 33 and "coined" it (June 2026). 68
  • Earlier: the 2020 summarization work was "the same three-stage approach" (January 2025). 59

His slides say InstructGPT made the recipe "canonical". 7

settled by Lambert, for KL Where did the KL penalty and feedback diagrams come from?

  • The chapter: Ziegler et al. formalized KL distances and feedback diagrams. 6 His 2023 talk also says 2019 is where those diagrams first appear. 57
  • KL is older, and he says so: Natasha Jaques's work on dialogue agents came before language models were popular, 69 as Chapter 15 confirms. 70 Ziegler's controller is itself "older than RLHF". 26
  • Diagrams stay open: the chapter's own figure is a 2017 loop, 3 and an April 2025 talk says the 2018 reward modeling paper has "one of these first" diagrams. 22

small Dates in this chapter are loose.

  • COACH comes right after TAMER as "concurrently or soon after", 13 yet the bibliography dates it 2017 and TAMER 2008. 71
  • The heading "2023 to the Present" holds ChatGPT and Constitutional AI, both 2022 in the table below, and he says "ChatGPT in 2022". 72 Likewise Gao et al., 2023, sits under "2019 to 2022".
  • A citation key isn't a date. rafailov2024direct is 2023, vonwerra2022trl is 2020 and lightman2023let is 2024. 73

Check yourself

TAMER and Christiano et al. both learn a reward from people. What changed, and why does it matter for language models?

TAMER's humans give each action a score, 17 and Christiano's choose between two trajectories. 18 Comparison is what he calls the fundamental assumption of RLHF, because comparing two things is easier than generating one. 5 Comparing two answers is how preference data works today. 16

What is the "environment" when RLHF runs on a language model?

There isn't one. The model generates a completion and stops, and a reward model gives one reward per sequence. 23 That is why he calls the setup contrived, and why it is striking that RL methods carry over. 27

Ziegler et al. published in 2019. Why does he date the excitement to around ChatGPT?

He gives no single cause. The paper was "much less known". 25 Its tasks and model capabilities differ from today's. 6 He says people reacted to the 2020 summarization work, yet it took almost three more years before they really realized it was a big thing. 29 What got him and many others excited was RLHF being linked to ChatGPT. 44

A colleague says ChatGPT is basically RLHF on top of GPT-3.5. What would Lambert correct?

RLHF was necessary but not sufficient, and pre-training is still most of the work. 46 OpenAI also says only that it used InstructGPT's methods with slight differences in data collection, 8 and he points out it doesn't say which. 45

Which parts of InstructGPT's recipe are still standard?

Fewer than you might expect, at least in what is known openly. He says little human demonstration data goes into SFT, guesses that synthetic data has a much bigger role, and says reward models aren't the key RL target any more. 9

Someone says the first RLHF on language models was 2020 summarization. How do you check?

Lambert has said so himself, in January 2025. 59 The book, slides and Lecture 10 say Ziegler et al. 2019. 6 56 58 Report both with their dates, then open the two papers from the table above.
