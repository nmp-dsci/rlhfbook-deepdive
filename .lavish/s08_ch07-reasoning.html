Chapter 7: Reasoning and Inference-Time Scaling

Reasoning models think for many tokens before they answer, and get better the more they think. They are trained with large amounts of RL with verifiable rewards (RLVR), plus RLHF. 1 Lambert calls the chapter historical context for how RLVR and inference-time scaling arrived. 2 It is about recipes and history, not new math.

The chapter in five points

  1. RLVR swaps the reward model for a checker. A function returns a positive reward for a correct answer and 0 otherwise. The rest is the RL of Chapter 6. 3
  2. The loop is simple. Sample many answers, step toward the correct ones, and repeat on the same data. The gains carry over to questions, and some domains, the model never saw. 4
  3. Little is new in principle. Rewarding correct answers is textbook RL. The new part is doing it without losing the model's general abilities. 5
  4. It arrived in 2025. o1 and DeepSeek R1 came first, and the industry followed. 6 The chapter lists over two dozen open reports from that year. 7
  5. The recipes are tricks, not a formula. Difficulty filtering, format rewards, length control and the rest are common, but rarely universal. 8 9

After this chapter you should be able to

  • Write a verifier for a math or code task, and say what it does and does not reward.
  • Explain why training-time RL and inference-time scaling are linked, and estimate pass@k.
  • Explain why prompts the model always or never solves are filtered out.
  • Name the common recipe choices of 2025 and which Lambert says are changing.

The role of RLVR

In Lecture 5: 1:33 · 3:09 · 4:44 · 5:16

The chapter opens with LeCun's 2016 cake: unsupervised learning is the cake, supervised learning the icing, RL the cherry. 10 Pretraining, SFT and RL now fill those roles, with RLHF as the precursor and RL for reasoning as the confirmation. 11 Whether RL is still the cherry is a point where Lambert's framing has shifted; see where the sources differ.

Example"Explain opportunity cost""Sum the primes below 20", or write fib(n)

The answer is pulled out with a regular expression, often from \boxed{}, "regardless of how the model arrived at it". 13 Code rewards are either all-or-nothing on the tests or partial credit. 14 The idea that opened the field was simple: we can scale training on problems where we already know the answer. 16

The name almost came out differently. The Tülu 3 work first called it RL with Ground Truth rewards (RLGT). "Verifiable" is broader, because a checker can accept many correct solutions, as unit tests do. 17 The slides add that the RLHF and RLVR split is "pedagogical, but in practice rare": real training mixes rewards, rubrics and tools. 18 19 Lambert also notes the verifier is becoming an environment again, closer to classical RL. 20

What the training loop does

Sample several answers to each question, step toward the correct ones, and repeat over the same questions. 4 The model "lightly" searches over behaviors, and RL raises the likelihood of the ones that correlate with correct answers. 23 Where SFT runs 1 or 2 epochs, RLVR can run hundreds or thousands over the same few problems. 24 The thinking itself can run to thousands of tokens, which is why long-context models came first. 25 Lambert describes these traces as "very odd" and repetitive, with some guess-and-check. 26

Why RL works now

In Lecture 5: 14:33 · 15:03 · 16:06

RL had a long reputation for being unstable and hard to reproduce. 27 The chapter credits the turnaround to two things: stability is solvable, and open tooling exists. 28 29 It also says reasoning RL only became viable with models from about 2024 on, so a capability floor was needed. 30 The lecture gives three reasons:

  • Infrastructure. In 2023 you "couldn't do RHF properly" without RL frameworks at scale. 31
  • Base models. Better base models, with mid-training on math and code, made it easier, and Qwen 2.5 became the default research model in January 2025. 32 The slides add that the math and code share of pretraining "bounds what RL can reach". 33
  • Clean evaluation. Math and code score training and evaluation the same way, unlike the gameable chat benchmarks before. 34

The 2025 talks say the same in other words: "stable implementation the base models being strong enough and just belief that things will work". 35

RL training and inference-time scaling

In Lecture 5: 5:46 · 6:17 · 8:19

Inference-time (or test-time) scaling means spending more compute at inference to do better. Methods existed before o1 and R1. 36 Performance grows with the log of the number of thinking tokens, and with the log of RL training compute, and the two are "deeply intertwined". 37 RL is a short path to inference-time scaling. Its length growth contrasts with early RLHF, where preference training made answers longer for marginal gains. 38 The extra tokens matter only if they improve performance, "otherwise it is just wasted energy". 39

Parallel test-time scaling samples several answers and combines them, most simply by majority vote, as in DeepSeek R1 and Phi-4. A trained selector was still uncommon in open recipes in 2026. 40

Try pass@k

Papers report pass@1, the accuracy of one sample, and pass@k, whether any of $k$ samples is right. The slides give the estimator for $n$ samples with $c$ correct. 41 Lambert calls his own explanation "a little waffling" and suggests asking a chatbot, 42 so here it is computed.

Estimate pass@k from n samples

pass@1
$= c/n$
pass@k, the slide's estimator
$= 1 - \binom{n-c}{k} / \binom{n}{k}$
Naive $1-(1-c/n)^k$

Before o1 and R1

In Lecture 5: 11:56 · 12:59 · 13:30

Earlier work trained on verifiable domains but either did not scale or gave up general performance for math and code. 43

  • STaR and Quiet-STaR (2022 onward). Sample rationales, keep the ones with correct answers, and train on them. 44 STaR approximates the policy gradient but filters samples and uses cross-entropy. 45
  • TRICE. An MCMC-inspired method. Lambert notes such complex algorithms tend not to scale, "which ended up being the chase" (case). 46
  • VinePPO. PPO with binary rewards on GSM8K and MATH. 47
  • Tülu 3. A simple PPO trainer rewarding correct answers without losing broad performance. 48 This paper coined "RLVR", and it was not a reasoning model. 49 Lambert thinks the term caught on "because it was so simple". 50

What came next was a reallocation of training stages and priorities rather than a new kind of RL. 6 The core step became either a large RL run or SFT on the outputs of a model that had one, which is distillation. 51

Common practices

In Lecture 5: 34:48 · 36:22 · 37:25 · 38:57 · 40:03 · 40:34

A model may use a trick without reporting it, so treat these as a reference, not a recipe. 8 Lambert adds that it is rare for every model to adopt any one trick, because model building is path dependent. 9

PracticeWhat and why
Offline difficulty filteringKeep prompts the starting model solves 20 to 80% of the time, measured by sampling about 16 completions each. 77 The lecture calls it one of the most unanimous tricks. 78
Online filtering, curriculaSkip prompts that became too easy, save hard ones for later. 79 Olmo 3's active sampling admits only groups with a nonzero gradient and refills the batch. 80 81
Remove the KL penaltyLonger runs and harder-to-hack rewards let many models drop it, to explore more. 82 Now contested; see below.
Relaxed clippingA higher upper clip bound, after DAPO, for exploration. Clipping can also cause spurious signals when rewards are imperfect. 83
Asynchronous updatesLong, highly variable completions leave compute idle. 84 Olmo 3's learner GPUs sat idle about 75% of the time. 85
Format and language rewardsSmall rewards for the <think> format, 86 and for not switching languages mid-trace. 87
Length controlGrow the length limit over training, so the model learns to reason within a budget first. 88 The lecture calls length control under-documented. 89
Loss normalizationNormalize losses or advantages over the batch, not the group. 90 Per-token losses are "a bit less biased". 91

Looking ahead

In Lecture 5: 42:39 · 43:43

The chapter expects some of these practices to be superseded. 94 It ends with RL "graduated from the 'cherry on top'" to "a load-bearing component of frontier model training". 95 The slides list the open questions: does RL discover capabilities or elicit them, how far reasoning goes without better pretraining data, and the future balance of pretraining and RL compute. 96 The recommended libraries also moved on: slime and SkyRL by mid-2026, after veRL and TRL. 97

Is RL just SFT with a regularizer?

A reader asked in Q&A 3. Lambert's answer is no, for two reasons. RL generates its own on-policy data, which "you can't just go back and generate" in advance for SFT. 98 And RL has negative gradients: it shows the model what not to do, as well as what to do. 99 He adds that many models, especially small ones, could get further with SFT alone than people think. 100

Where this is in the code

Chapter 7 has no code of its own, but the policy-gradient code from Chapter 6 implements several of its practices. It trains on reasoning-gym's word-reversal task. 101 I did not run it.

  • Format reward: 0.25 per tag, weighted 0.5 and added to correctness. 21 22 Answers are extracted by reasoning-gym's own function, which I have not read. 102
  • Zero-gradient filtering: the DAPO config drops any group whose completions are all right or all wrong. 103
  • Clip-higher, no KL and an overlong penalty: the DAPO config sets the upper clip to 0.28, beta to 0, and a soft penalty over the last 128 of 512 tokens. 104 105
def _format_reward(completions: list[str]) -> list[float]:
    """Compute format reward based on presence of thinking/answer tags."""

    def count_tags(text: str) -> float:
        count = 0.0
        if re.search(r"\s*<think>\s*", text):
            count += 0.25
        if re.search(r"\s*</think>\s*", text):
            count += 0.25
        if re.search(r"\s*<answer>\s*", text):
            count += 0.25
        if re.search(r"\s*</answer>\s*", text):
            count += 0.25
        return count

    return [count_tags(c) for c in completions]


def compute_rewards(
    entries: list[dict],
    completions: list[str],
    lengths: list[int],
    dataset: ProceduralDataset,
    cfg: Config,
    device: torch.device,
) -> TensorDict:
    """Compute reward components for a rollout group as a single TensorDict.

    Keys: ``total``, ``correctness``, ``format``, ``penalty``, ``binary``.
    """
    correctness = _correctness_reward(dataset, completions, entries)
    penalty = _response_penalties(lengths, cfg)
    format = _format_reward(completions)
    total = [
        c + p + cfg.format_weight * f for c, p, f in zip(correctness, penalty, format, strict=True)

Is the KL penalty gone for RLVR?

  • Talk (Apr 2025): in Tülu 3 sweeps of β from 0.1 to 0.001, the KL setting "doesn't really matter". 108

Check yourself

Your model solves a training prompt in all 16 of 16 samples. What does GRPO learn from it?

Nothing. Every completion gets the same reward, so there is no gradient between them, which is why such prompts are filtered out. 77 80

A completion reaches 77 through wrong reasoning. What does the verifier give it?

Full reward. The extracted answer is checked "regardless of how the model arrived at it". 13

Why add a format reward if it says nothing about correctness?

To make the reasoning process predictable, with a <think> block and an extractable answer. 86

Why start RL with a short length limit and raise it later?

So the model first learns to reason within a limited budget, then uses those habits efficiently on harder problems. It fights overthinking. 88

Why could early RLVR drop the KL penalty that RLHF needs?

RLHF's learned reward model can be over-optimized, while a verifier is precise and harder to game. 15 82 Lambert expects it may return for very long runs. 109

A friend says RL is just SFT with extra steps. What two things does RL have?

On-policy data that could not be prepared in advance, and negative gradients that push down wrong answers. 98 99

Agent notes

agent note pass@k is not $1-(1-p)^k$. Not Lambert: the slide's estimator draws $k$ of the $n$ samples without replacement. 41 The naive formula treats every attempt as independent, with success rate $c/n$. With $n = 16$, $c = 4$ and $k = 4$, the estimator gives 0.728 and the naive formula 0.684. They agree only as $n$ grows large relative to $k$. When $k = n$, the estimator is exactly 1 if any sample is correct.

agent note Why 20 to 80%. Not Lambert: with binary rewards and $G$ independent samples, a group is useless with probability $p^G + (1-p)^G$. With 8 samples that is 0.8% at a 50% solve rate, but 17% at 80% and 43% at 90%. The chapter's band keeps that waste small. The arithmetic is mine; the band and its reason are the chapter's. 77

agent note The date gaps are probably two kinds of date. Not Lambert: the chapter's table is captioned as technical reports, 7 while the slides date model releases. That would explain table dates that are a few days to six weeks later. I have not checked the papers.
