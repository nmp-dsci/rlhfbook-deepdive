Chapter 8: Direct Alignment Algorithms

Direct alignment algorithms (DAAs) solve the same RLHF objective as Chapters 5 and 6, on the same preference data, without training a reward model or running RL. 1 2 DPO is the one that mattered. It turns the KL-constrained objective into a single loss on chosen and rejected completions, 3 and Lambert's lecture walks through every step of the derivation, because the paper skips some. 4

The chapter in five points

  1. The RLHF objective has a closed-form optimum. Invert it, and the reward becomes a log-ratio of the policy to the reference. 5 6
  2. DPO trains a reward model in disguise. The paper's subtitle is "Your Language Model is Secretly a Reward Model". 7
  3. The loss is a few lines. Log-probabilities from the policy and a frozen reference, a difference, and a log-sigmoid. 8 9
  4. Variants abound, but data matters more. The choice of algorithm is "far less important than the initial model and the data used". 10
  5. It is offline, and a hair behind RL. Without fresh completions, DPO trails online RL, yet it stays popular for its simplicity. 11 12

After this chapter you should be able to

  • Derive the DPO loss from the KL-constrained RLHF objective, and say where $Z(x)$ goes.
  • Read a DPO training log: loss, accuracy, margins and the two implicit rewards.
  • Explain likelihood displacement, and why the loss can fall while the chosen answer gets less likely.
  • Say what IPO, cDPO, ODPO, ORPO and SimPO change, and which drop the reference model.
  • Explain the offline versus online argument, and Lambert's current view of where DPO fits.

Why DPO mattered

In Lecture 6: 1:00 · 1:31 · 2:02 · 3:36

In May 2023 most open "aligned" models were only SFT, and open RLHF pipelines were struggling. 13 Lambert says most of them "crashed and burned", and DPO made post-training "far more accessible". 14 Its biggest impact was lowering the barrier to entry: less compute, simpler to write, easier to get working. 15 16 He still rates it a big deal. 17 In December 2023 he called the paper "exceedingly clever", 18 and in 2024 he said DPO "is where everyone will start" in alignment research, for good reason. 19

It did not work at once. The community needed months to find the right data and hyperparameters, notably "surprisingly low learning rates". 20 21 Zephyr-β kicked it off in October 2023, and Llama 3 Instruct, Tülu 2 and 3, Nemotron 4 340B and others followed. 20 SLiC-HF came first, but never caught on. 22 The release set off a long, meme-filled DPO versus RL debate, 23 which Lambert was asked about at every conference "for like a year straight". His answer is both. 24

"Direct alignment algorithms" is Lambert's name for the family of methods that train like DPO on static preference data. He admits it "didn't stick much". 25 26

How DPO works

In Lecture 6: 5:10 · 7:16 · 28:34 · 29:37

The promise: learn from the data directly, with no RL loop and no separate reward model. 29 The loss compares how much the policy has shifted the chosen and rejected completions relative to a reference model:

$$\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_\theta;\pi_{\text{ref}}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x,y_c,y_r)\sim\mathcal{D}}\Big[\log\sigma\Big(\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_c\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_c\mid x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y_r\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_r\mid x)}\Big)\Big]$$
TermMeaning
$x,\ y_c,\ y_r$A prompt, its chosen completion and its rejected completion: the pairwise data of Chapter 5. 2
$\pi_\theta$, $\pi_{\text{ref}}$The model being trained, and a frozen copy of where it started.
$\beta\log\frac{\pi_\theta(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)}$The implicit reward, a log-ratio that replaces an external reward model. 6 Each $\log\pi(y\mid x)$ is a sum of token log-probabilities over the completion. 30
$\beta$Balances reward against KL distance from the reference, so it controls over-optimization. 31 Large β keeps the policy close; small β lets it move and over-optimize. 32
$\sigma$The sigmoid of Bradley-Terry. The loss falls when the chosen shift exceeds the rejected shift. 33

Its gradient shows what each step does:

$$\nabla_\theta\mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}} = -\beta\,\mathbb{E}\Big[\underbrace{\sigma\big(r_\theta(x,y_r) - r_\theta(x,y_c)\big)}_{w,\ \text{how wrong}}\big(\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_c\mid x) - \nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_r\mid x)\big)\Big]$$

The weight $w$ runs from 0 to 1 and is larger when the implicit reward ranks the pair wrongly. The bracket raises the chosen completion and lowers the rejected one, and β scales the step. 34 35 You never write this gradient yourself: you implement the loss, and autograd does the rest. 36

Try "Both down". The loss is lower than at the start, yet the chosen completion is far less likely than before. That is likelihood displacement, covered below. "Wrong order" pushes $w$ toward 1, the biggest corrective step.

Because the loss is directly differentiable, DPO gets an exact gradient instead of estimating one by sampling and scoring. 37 It solves for the exact policy at a given KL strength, because its data is not generated online. That makes β easier to tune than in online RL, but its best value still depends on the model and data. 38

The derivation, in four parts

In Lecture 6: 7:48 · 13:32 · 17:40 · 19:15 · 21:19 · 24:24 · 25:58

The chapter takes two steps: find the policy that solves the RLHF objective, then fit it to pairwise preferences. 5 The slides split that into four. 39

1. Solve the objective for the optimal policy

Start from the objective, write the KL term as an expectation, flip max to min and divide by β:

$$\max_\pi\ \mathbb{E}\big[r(x,y)\big] - \beta\,\mathcal{D}_{\text{KL}}\big(\pi\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}\big) \;\;\Longleftrightarrow\;\; \min_\pi\ \mathbb{E}_{x}\,\mathbb{E}_{y\sim\pi}\Big[\log\frac{\pi(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} - \frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Big]$$

This almost looks like a KL divergence from $\pi_{\text{ref}}\,e^{r/\beta}$, except that target is not a distribution: it does not sum to 1. 40 Before Gibbs' inequality can be used, a partition function is introduced. 41 It normalizes the target: 42

$$Z(x) = \sum_y \pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\Big(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Big)$$

Adding and subtracting $\log Z(x)$ turns the objective into a true KL divergence minus $\log Z(x)$, which does not depend on $\pi$. A KL divergence is zero only when the two distributions are equal, by Gibbs' inequality. 43 So:

$$\pi^*(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\exp\Big(\frac{1}{\beta}r(x,y)\Big)$$

Lambert calls this the core of the paper: "You can just go towards the optimal solution", an RL problem solved with a direct gradient. 44

2. Invert it for the reward

Take logs of both sides and solve for $r$: 45

$$r^*(x,y) = \beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)} + \beta\log Z(x)$$

This is the title of the paper. DPO does not train a separate reward model; the language model is an implicit one, 46 so we can "bypass learning an explicit reward model entirely". 47

3. Plug it into Bradley-Terry

Substitute $r^*$ into $p^*(y_1 \succ y_2\mid x) = \frac{e^{r^*(x,y_1)}}{e^{r^*(x,y_1)} + e^{r^*(x,y_2)}}$. Every term carries the same $e^{\beta\log Z(x)}$, so it cancels, 48 and the "intractable term is gone". 49 Dividing through gives a sigmoid:

$$p^*(y_1\succ y_2\mid x) = \sigma\Big(\beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y_1\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_1\mid x)} - \beta\log\frac{\pi^*(y_2\mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_2\mid x)}\Big)$$

4. Read off the loss and gradient

Minimize the negative log-likelihood of the observed preferences, as for a Bradley-Terry reward model, 50 with the trainable $\pi_\theta$ in place of $\pi^*$. That gives the loss above: "no reward model, no sampling, no RL loop". 51 The gradient follows from $\sigma'(u) = \sigma(u)(1-\sigma(u))$ and $1-\sigma(u) = \sigma(-u)$. 52 The slides sum it up: the reward model "was hiding inside the policy the whole time". 53 The paper also derives a Plackett-Luce version, which is rarely used. 54

Try the closed form

This is $\pi^*$ for one prompt with four made-up completions. Lower β moves probability toward high reward and away from the reference. The last two columns are always equal. That is step 2: the implicit reward is the true reward shifted by $\beta\log Z$, the same shift for every completion, and step 3 cancels it.

Weaknesses and variants

In Lecture 6: 33:49 · 34:19 · 35:21 · 35:52

With no reward model rating rollouts, DPO treats every preference pair as equally strong, even though richer labels exist. 55 Each variant tweaks the loss to fix one limitation, "often a one-line change". 56

Of the reference-free ones, Lambert says ORPO "didn't take off", while SimPO "worked a little bit better". 73 He could make "eight slides" of variants, but "most of what matters ends up being the data". 74 75 Scott Geng, in Conversation 2, agrees that data matters more than the objective, 76 and finds the original DPO loss "still performs very well" if tuned. 77 Back in December 2023 he said the same of IPO and cDPO: they do not answer the fundamental questions. 78

Likelihood displacement

Lambert calls it "one of the most interesting things in DPO": the loss only pushes up the margin. 79 So the model can lower it by pushing the rejected probability down faster than the chosen, while the chosen probability also falls. 80 81 The chapter cites work suggesting this raises the probability of behaviors outside the training data, and methods such as Cal-DPO and AlphaPO that mitigate it. Its exact impact is "not well known". 82 Lambert's worry in the lecture is the side effects "when it's out of distribution". 83 The chapter's sketch shows it.

One common fix is an SFT term on the chosen response. 81 84 How much displacement matters in practice is one of the open questions.

Offline data

The other reason DPO may have a lower ceiling is that its training signal comes from completions by older or other models. Online variants generate fresh completions during training. 72 This is the core of the DPO versus RL question.

Implementation

In Lecture 6: 32:16 · 34:19 · 36:23

The DPO loss fits into a standard training stack, because the log-probabilities it needs come from a normal forward pass, plus a reference model. 8 Here it is in the book's code. Summed log-probabilities for DPO, averaged ones for SimPO and DPO-Norm, 85 then the loss, with label smoothing for cDPO: 9

        # Compute log ratios (implicit rewards)
        chosen_logratios = policy_chosen_logps - ref_chosen_logps
        rejected_logratios = policy_rejected_logps - ref_rejected_logps

        # DPO logits: difference in log ratios
        logits = chosen_logratios - rejected_logratios

        # cDPO: label smoothing for noisy preferences
        if self.label_smoothing > 0:
            # Soft labels: [1-eps, eps] instead of [1, 0]
            # This translates to: (1-eps) * log(sigmoid(x)) + eps * log(sigmoid(-x))
            # = (1-2*eps) * log(sigmoid(x)) + eps * log(sigmoid(x)) + eps * log(sigmoid(-x))
            # = (1-2*eps) * log(sigmoid(x)) + eps * log(1)  (since sigmoid(x) + sigmoid(-x) = 1)
            # Simplified: just use soft cross-entropy
            losses = (
                -F.logsigmoid(self.beta * logits) * (1 - self.label_smoothing)
                - F.logsigmoid(-self.beta * logits) * self.label_smoothing
            )
        else:
            # Standard DPO
            losses = -F.logsigmoid(self.beta * logits)

        # Compute implicit rewards for logging
        chosen_rewards = self.beta * chosen_logratios.detach()
        rejected_rewards = self.beta * rejected_logratios.detach()

        metrics = {
            "chosen_rewards": chosen_rewards.mean().item(),
            "rejected_rewards": rejected_rewards.mean().item(),
            "margins": (chosen_rewards - rejected_rewards).mean().item(),
            "accuracy": (chosen_rewards > rejected_rewards).float().mean().item(),
        }

        return losses.mean(), metrics

The logged "rewards" are β times the detached log-ratios, and "accuracy" is the share of pairs where the chosen implicit reward is higher. 86 Two points from the chapter:

  • The KL is static. DPO steps toward the optimal solution for the data at a fixed β, while RL steps based on the current batch. 87 The lecture: the optimal gradient has "a targeted KL distance built into it", 88 and dynamic KL controllers from RL "aren't really suited or possible" in DPO. 89
  • Cache the reference. Running policy and reference together doubles memory. Precompute the reference log-probabilities instead, for 50% less peak memory. 90 91 Otherwise the reference model is a fixed cost: memory at training time, or a precomputation. 92

The book's code does not cache: it runs the frozen reference on every batch, 93 and lists caching as a to-do. 94 SimPO and ORPO skip the reference model. 95

Scale brings its own problems. In Conversation 2, Scott Geng describes the Olmo 3 32B DPO run. Running chosen and rejected as two separate forward passes, instead of one concatenated pass, "would like just break the model for some reason". 96

DAAs with synthetic preference data

In Lecture 6: 36:55 · 37:26 · 37:58 · 39:01

Most popular DAA datasets are synthetic: a frontier model judges which of two completions wins, 97 since these methods need feedback data, not necessarily human feedback. 98 UltraFeedback came first. The lecture describes it as about 10,000 prompts, 99 and Tülu 3 scaled the method to 100,000 and 200,000. 100 The chapter recommends this setup as the place most practitioners should start. 101

Which completions go into the pairs has been argued two ways:

  • On-policy (Tülu 3, 2024). Include completions from the model you are tuning, mixed into a larger pool, so the contrastive loss works on the tokens the model actually generates. 102 103
  • Delta learning (Olmo 3 and SmolLM 3, 2025). The gap between chosen and rejected matters more than which models wrote them. Both teams paired Qwen 3 32B chosen against Qwen 3 0.6B rejected. 104 105 If all the answers are too similar, the models learn less. 106

Conversation 2 has the story behind delta learning. Scott Geng found that SFT on UltraFeedback's chosen responses would "obliterate" a newer model, while preference tuning on the same responses helped. 107 A weak chosen response still teaches "if you contrast it with something that's even worse". 108 The paper pushed this to two models weaker than the one being trained, 3B against 1.5B. 109 Back in December 2023, Lambert had asked what happens when the chosen and rejected are "both good or both bad". 110

For Olmo 3's reasoning model the recipe barely changed: generate the pairs with Qwen 3 with thinking switched on. 111 The data has quirks. In one pair the 32B model wrote a crude creative-writing request and the 0.6B model refused, so the refusal became the rejected answer. 112 Mixing prompts was "random": Geng suspects offline DPO can overfit and reward-hack in ways SFT does not. 113 The chapter adds another bias: LLM judges favor long outputs and their own style. 114

The chapter's last point: tokens get different gradients according to how much they contributed to the margin. 115

Check yourself

The policy equals the reference. What are the DPO loss and the weight $w$?

Both log-ratios are 0, so $u = 0$, the loss is $\log 2 \approx 0.693$ and $w = 0.5$. Try "Start" in the widget. This is why early DPO loss sits near 0.69. 131

Why does the partition function $Z(x)$, a sum over all possible completions, never need computing?

It depends only on the prompt, so it adds the same $\beta\log Z(x)$ to the reward of every completion, and cancels in the Bradley-Terry ratio. 48 49

The loss fell, and the chosen completion's log-probability also fell. Is something broken?

Not necessarily. The loss rewards only the margin, so both can fall as long as the rejected falls further. That is likelihood displacement. 80 Whether it matters is debated. 146 82

What does β do, and why is DPO's KL called static?

β trades reward against distance from the reference: large β stays close, small β can over-optimize. 32 DPO steps to the optimum for that β on fixed data, while RL steps on fresh batches. 87

Which variants drop the reference model, and what do they lose?

ORPO and SimPO. 68 71 95 Both are more sensitive to log-probability scaling and learning rate, 165 and the code marks both as still being retuned. 69

Why is SFT on a weak chosen response harmful, while DPO on the same pair helps?

SFT imitates the chosen response. DPO learns from the gap between chosen and rejected, which can teach even when both are weak. 108 104

Agent notes

agent note The two βs in the gradient are both right. Not Lambert: inside the sigmoid, β is part of each implicit reward. Outside, a second β comes from the chain rule: $\nabla_\theta u = \beta(\nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_c\mid x) - \nabla_\theta\log\pi_\theta(y_r\mid x))$. The chapter's equation and the slide both have this form. 52 35 So the lecture's worry 164 seems unfounded.

agent note Why $\pi^*$ is not something you can compute. Not Lambert: $Z(x)$ sums over every possible completion, which is intractable for a language model. That is why the derivation must cancel it 49 rather than evaluate it. The four-completion widget can compute $Z$ only because its space is tiny.

agent note On "mediated through the partition function". The slide ties displacement to $Z(x)$, 81 and the lecture says it "comes down to this partition function". 166 The chapter does not make that link. 80 My reading, not Lambert's: the loss, with $Z$ cancelled, constrains only the gap between two completions, so probability mass is free to move to completions outside the data. I have not read the cited papers.

agent note Summed log-probabilities favor short answers. Not Lambert: a sum over tokens is more negative for longer completions, which is what the averaging in SimPO and DPO-Norm addresses. 85 70 With summed log-ratios, a shift of tens of nats across a long completion is ordinary, which is why the widget's sliders reach ±40.
