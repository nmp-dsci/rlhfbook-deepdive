Chapter 15: Regularization

Every post-training method is strong enough to pull the model too far from the general model it started as, and when out-of-distribution performance drops, that is over-optimization. 1 This chapter covers the tools that hold the model back. 2 Lambert calls them "a solution to lecture 9", 3 which is Chapter 14. The main tool is a KL penalty to a reference model. 4 5 The second half is about implicit regularization: RL's on-policy structure makes it forget less than SFT, even with no penalty at all. 6 7

The chapter in five points

  1. The KL penalty subtracts $\lambda\,\mathrm{KL}(\pi_{\text{RL}}\|\pi_{\text{ref}})$ from the reward; it is a reverse KL, estimated on the policy's own samples. 8 9
  2. Other explicit tools (pretraining gradients, NLL in DPO, margin losses) tend to be scaffolding that the next model drops. 10 11
  3. SFT memorizes, RL generalizes: on a rule shift, RL improves and SFT collapses. 12 13
  4. SFT is forward KL, KL-regularized RL is reverse KL, and for a multimodal model that makes RL forget less. 14 15
  5. RL's Razor: forgetting tracks KL drift, and on-policy data, not negative gradients, explains RL's smaller drift. 16 17

After this chapter you should be able to

  • Write the KL-penalized reward and say which direction of KL it is.
  • Estimate a KL from samples, and say why it is called a distance when it is not one.
  • Derive why SFT minimizes forward KL and KL-regularized RL minimizes reverse KL.
  • Explain why mode-seeking RL forgets less, against the naive reading.
  • Say why agentic RL recipes in 2026 drop the KL penalty, and what replaces it.

Why regularize

In Lecture 10: 00:00 · 00:30 · 01:02 · 01:33 · 02:03

Lambert frames Lecture 10 as "another math lecture" on why "RL generalizes better, SFT forgets more". 18 The job is "how to control your model from changing too much during a single RL run". 19 In the chapter's words, over-optimization in this setting looks like "nonsensical text": math reasoning you can follow that lands on a badly wrong answer, repeated text, switched languages, excess special characters. 2 (Chapter 14 described subtler failures; the Chapter 14 page compares the two.)

The general form subtracts a weighted regularizer from the reward, and the reference implementation makes that regularizer a KL divergence: 8 20

$$ r = r_\theta - \lambda\, r_{\text{reg.}} $$

$$ r = r_\theta - \lambda_{\text{KL}}\, \mathcal{D}_{\text{KL}}\!\left(\pi_{\text{RL}}(y \mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)\right) $$

  • $r_\theta$ is the reward model's score, and $r$ is the reward RL actually optimizes. 8
  • $\pi_{\text{RL}}$ is the policy being trained; $\pi_{\text{ref}}$ is a frozen copy from before RL, usually the instruction-tuned model, sometimes an earlier RL checkpoint. 21 5
  • $\lambda_{\text{KL}}$ weighs the penalty. In practice it is usually written $\beta$; 22 Lambert asks viewers to "track down the literature" on when and why both letters are used. 23

Other regularizers come and go: they stabilize one setup and are simplified away in the next generation. 10 What lasts is the KL penalty, which the chapter calls "the most popular variant" as of 2026. 4 The lecture adds that it "came out of vogue in the RLVR era". 24 See disagreements.

The KL penalty

In Lecture 10: 02:36 · 03:08 · 03:39 · 04:10 · 04:40 · 05:10 · 05:41

KL divergence measures how far one distribution has drifted from another; it is zero when they produce identical outputs. 25 26

$$ \mathcal{D}_{\text{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q) = \sum_{x} P(x)\log\frac{P(x)}{Q(x)} $$

Here $P$ is the new model and $Q$ the reference. 9 The penalty is the reverse KL to the reference: sample tokens from the RL model, score them under the reference. 9 27 28 It punishes the policy hard for putting mass where the reference puts little. 29 22 The other direction appears inside the trust regions of some RL algorithms and is closer to distillation or behavior cloning. 30 Lecture 0: it is "reverse because we're kind of sampling from the student rather than the target". 31

The idea predates language models. Natasha Jaques used KL control for dialogue agents, and it became a core technique for fine-tuning pretrained models. 32 33 The reference's job, in the chapter's words: the starting model "has a style that you would like to stay close to". 21 Lecture 3 puts it as the "clearest way to prevent what is called over optimization". 34

"Distance" is a loose word

KL is not a distance: it is asymmetric, so it is not a metric. 35 36 People still say "KL distance", meaning the optimization distance, how far the model has moved. 37 38 On the Q&A 2 slide he says he "battled my editors on this too". 39 The book's own definition says "the KL distance from $Q$ to $P$". 26

Measuring and controlling it

In Lecture 10: 05:41 · 06:14 · 06:46 · 07:17 · 07:47 · 08:18 · 08:50 · 09:20 · 09:50 · 10:22

Sampling from $P$ turns the sum into an expectation you can estimate from log-probabilities: 40

$$ \mathcal{D}_{\text{KL}}(P\,\|\,Q) = \mathbb{E}_{x\sim P}\left[\log P(x) - \log Q(x)\right] $$

The chapter's snippet generates a sequence, runs both models over it, and sums the token log-probabilities: the difference of the sums approximates KL. 41 Lambert on the slide version: "I don't love this code that Claude wrote for me here". 42 In practice there are lower-variance estimators, $k_1$, $k_2$ and $k_3$, from John Schulman's note, and "you'll switch between them depending on which estimate is stable". 43 44

Practitioners watch the KL curve every run, because a very large KL "usually means a bug or a broken model". 43 45 The slide's example is an OLMo-2-7B GRPO run trained straight from the base model: reward climbs while KL rises and wanders, with β = 0, and "This is a healthy shape". 46 47 You can watch KL with no penalty on.

β began as a controller

The first RLHF-on-language-models paper (Ziegler et al. 2019) did not fix β. It set a target KL and adjusted β with a "log-space proportional controller": 48 49

$$ e_t = \operatorname{clip}\!\left(\frac{\mathrm{KL}(\pi_t,\pi_{\text{ref}}) - \mathrm{KL}_{\text{target}}}{\mathrm{KL}_{\text{target}}},\,-0.2,\,0.2\right) $$

$$ \beta_{t+1} = \beta_t\,(1 + K_\beta\, e_t) $$

  • $e_t$ is the clipped relative error between the measured KL and the target.
  • When KL runs above target, $e_t > 0$ and β grows, pulling the policy back; below target β shrinks. $K_\beta = 0.1$. 48
  • Runs with different seeds land on the same KL budget, so they are comparable. 48

PPO's original paper had an adaptive variant that doubled or halved β, and constrained RL later used full PID controllers. 50 Modern practice "swung back" to a small static β, or none. 48 51 52 TRL's controller was deleted in its rewrite; open-instruct uses a static beta = 0.05. 53

The penalty in code

In the book's policy-gradient code, the reward-side penalty is one function. It runs during rollouts, before advantages are computed: 54 55

def apply_reward_kl(
    rewards: TensorDict,
    log_probs: torch.Tensor,
    log_probs_ref: torch.Tensor,
    action_mask: torch.Tensor,
    cfg: Config,
) -> TensorDict:
    """Apply KL penalty to the combined ``total`` reward (REINFORCE/RLOO/PPO)."""
    if not cfg.beta or cfg.loss not in ["ppo", "rloo", "reinforce"]:
        return rewards
    device = rewards["total"].device
    log_probs = log_probs.to(device)
    log_probs_ref = log_probs_ref.to(device)
    action_mask = action_mask.to(device)
    kl_div = get_approx_kl(cfg.kl_estimator, log_probs, log_probs_ref, action_mask)
    kl_div = masked_mean(kl_div, mask=action_mask, dim=-1, keepdim=True)
    rewards["total"] = rewards["total"] - cfg.beta * kl_div
    return rewards

Three things to notice:

  • It is exactly the chapter's equation. The total reward minus cfg.beta times an estimated KL. 56 8
  • The KL is per token, then averaged over the completion with masked_mean. 56 Lecture 3: "KL penalties are token level". 57 The chapter's snippet sums to a sequence instead; see the traps.
  • It only applies to PPO, RLOO and REINFORCE. 55 GRPO puts its KL term in the loss rather than the reward, 58 59 which is the optional beta * kl_loss shown on the Chapter 14 page.

The default estimator is kl3, which estimates $\mathrm{KL}(\pi\|\pi_{\text{ref}})$ from the policy's own samples; kl1 is the plain $\log(\pi/\pi_{\text{ref}})$. 60 61 The default beta is 0.0, so the penalty is off unless you turn it on. 62 The math is on the Chapter 6 page. I have not run it.

Other tools to control optimization

In Lecture 10: 24:55 · 25:27 · 25:57 · 26:27 · 26:59

The chapter includes these to show how leading models got stable optimization in their setups, "rather than as tools that should work explicitly in every setup". 63 The slides call most of them "scaffolding". 11

Pretraining gradients (InstructGPT)

Keep the model close to a dataset: InstructGPT added a next-token loss on pretraining documents "to fix the performance regressions on public NLP datasets". 64 65

$$ J(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}_{\pi_{\text{RL},\theta}}}\!\left[r_\theta(y\mid x) - \lambda\, r_{\text{reg.}}\right] + \gamma\, \mathbb{E}_{x\sim\mathcal{D}_{\text{pretrain}}}\!\left[\log \pi_{\text{RL},\theta}(x)\right] $$

The first term is the regularized RL reward on the policy's own samples; the second rewards high likelihood on pretraining text, weighted by $\gamma$. 65 Lambert: "Haven't seen stuff like this recently." 66

NLL alongside DPO

Add a negative log-likelihood term to DPO; the same trick works for reward models. 67

$$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO+NLL}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}} + \alpha\, \mathcal{L}_{\text{NLL}}, \qquad \mathcal{L}_{\text{NLL}} = -\frac{\log P_\theta(c^w, y^w\mid x)}{|c^w| + |y^w|} $$

The DPO term widens the margin between the winner and the loser, with $\beta$ setting how hard it pulls from the reference. The NLL term, weighted by $\alpha$ and normalized by length, keeps the winner likely "in an absolute language modeling sense" and not just better than the loser. 68 The book's code has a relative of this: ORPO's loss is an NLL term on the chosen answer minus a log-odds term. 69 See the Chapter 8 page.

Margins in reward modeling

Most reward models have no regularization beyond the contrastive loss, and direct alignment algorithms regularize through $\beta$. 70 Llama 2 added a margin from annotator rating gaps: 71

$$ \mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\log\sigma\!\left(r_\theta(y_c\mid x) - r_\theta(y_r\mid x) - m(y_c,y_r)\right) $$

Here $m$ is how far apart two annotators' ratings put the chosen $y_c$ and rejected $y_r$, so a clear win must be won by more. 71 Reward-weighted DPO, RPO and REBEL use reward margins too. 72 See the Chapter 5 page.

Why these fade: "you want to revisit your recipe and make it simpler for the future model versions because the simpler ideas normally scale better". 73

SFT is forward KL, RL is reverse KL

In Lecture 10: 10:54 · 11:25 · 11:56 · 12:26 · 12:57 · 13:28 · 13:59 · 14:31 · 15:02 · 15:33 · 16:05 · 16:35 · 17:07 · 17:38 · 18:09 · 18:40 · 19:11 · 19:41 · 20:12

Why does RL forget less? The chapter's answer: the two methods minimize the two directions of KL, and the direction is set by where the samples come from. 78 79 (Here $P$ is the target and $Q$ the model, the reverse of the letters above.) 80 Forward is "the canonical way"; reverse is the other one. 81

SFT minimizes forward KL

$$ \mathrm{KL}(\pi_\star\|\pi_\theta) = \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\log\pi_\star(y\mid x)\right]}_{-H(\pi_\star),\ \text{constant}} \;-\; \underbrace{\mathbb{E}_{(x,y)\sim\mathcal{D}}\left[\log\pi_\theta(y\mid x)\right]}_{-\mathcal{L}_{\text{SFT}}(\theta)} $$

The samples are the dataset. The first term is the data's negative entropy, which does not depend on $\theta$; the second is the SFT cross-entropy loss. Same gradients, same minimum. 82 83 84

KL-regularized RL minimizes reverse KL

Start from the RL objective with the penalty, divide by $-\beta$, and normalize with a partition function $Z(x)$. The objective becomes a single KL, minimized at the reward-tilted reference: 85 86

$$ \pi_\star(y\mid x) = \frac{1}{Z(x)}\,\pi_{\text{ref}}(y\mid x)\,\exp\!\left(\tfrac{1}{\beta}\,r(x,y)\right) $$

Take the reference, multiply each completion's probability by $e^{r/\beta}$, renormalize. Large $\beta$ barely tilts; small $\beta$ piles mass on the highest-reward completions. 87 88 It is the same $\pi_\star$ that DPO starts from. 89 The name came from AI models: a "claudism", he says. 90 87 Try it:

Expanding $\mathrm{KL}(\pi_\theta\|\pi_\star)$ and dropping the constant $\log Z$ gives back $-\tfrac{1}{\beta}\mathcal{J}_{\text{RL}}$, so maximizing the RL objective is minimizing the reverse KL to $\pi_\star$. 14 91 The lecture adds a caveat: "if you have beta equal to zero, this theory on the shape of the KL does not apply". 92 79 He also warns that going "from a definition to some implementation" risks dropping assumptions. 93 Pairing the two is "a little cute", since one is reward optimization and the other learning from data. 94

The naive read, and why it flips

If forward KL covers every mode, shouldn't SFT preserve old knowledge, and mode-seeking RL abandon it? 15 104 Here is that intuition on a toy, where the "model" is a single bell curve:

The chapter says "the opposite holds": the intuition assumes a unimodal policy, and LLMs have many modes. 15 105 Chen et al. 2025 picture a policy with an "old" mode of prior knowledge and a "new" mode for the task. 106 Forward KL stretches the policy to cover the target and pulls mass away from the old mode; reverse KL shifts the new mode toward the target and leaves the old one alone. 107 108 109 The chapter's figure. Training only on its own generations keeps RL's updates local to where it already puts mass. 110

Lambert in the lecture: "this intuition is backwards", partly because "the words being used are pretty bad". 111 Of the figure: "not perfectly intuitive but it gets the point across", with mass covering as "blanketing" and mode seeking as "moving over". 112 His takeaway: reverse KL's mode seeking "is better suited" than SFT, which "has to kind of try to memorize things". 113

RL's Razor: why online RL forgets less

In Lecture 10: 24:22 · 24:55

Many policies solve a new task. Shenfeld et al. 2026 propose that "on-policy methods such as RL are inherently biased toward solutions that remain closer to the original policy in KL divergence". 114 The chapter's figure.

$$ \text{Forgetting} \approx f\!\left(\mathbb{E}_{x\sim\tau}\left[\mathrm{KL}\!\left(\pi_0(\cdot\mid x)\,\|\,\pi(\cdot\mid x)\right)\right]\right) $$

  • $\pi_0$ is the initial model, $\pi$ the fine-tuned one, and $\tau$ the new task's inputs. 16
  • Across RL and SFT variants, forgetting correlates with this KL at $R^2 = 0.96$, though it is measured on new-task data, not on the old tasks. 16 115
  • So it is "a cheap forgetting predictor". 115

What makes RL's drift smaller? The authors split RL from SFT along two axes, on-policy data and negative gradients, and find on-policy data "fully accounts for the difference"; negative gradients have "no discernible effect". 17 115 On-policy samples are things the model already finds likely, so each update stays near the current distribution; SFT's targets can be arbitrarily far away. 116 Lambert's summary: RL policies "are biased towards ones that are a bit simpler" and forget less, though his verdict on the results is "probably okay". 117 118

Is RL just regularized SFT?

A reader asked this in Q&A 3. Lambert: the losses share their core term, and SFT is the "all-positive" special case. 119 But "you can't just go back and generate that data a priori such that you could only do SFT". 120 SFT is the foundation, and RL is how the model "learns a lot more from there". 121 122 He names one difference as "huge": RL has negative gradients, showing the model "what it should not do". 123 119 That sits awkwardly with RL's Razor; see disagreements.

2026: the KL penalty is moving on

In Lecture 10: 27:32 · 28:03 · 28:35 · 29:07 · 29:38 · 30:10

"A lot of the recent models are not using KL penalties." 124

  • GLM-5 removes it "to accelerate RL improvement". 125
  • Kimi, per the Lecture 10 slide, ships K2 and K3 with no KL penalty and no reference policy. 125 Q&A 3 is less sure; see disagreements.
  • TMax, Lambert's terminal-agent work: a small KL made collapse less severe but lowered reward, so the final recipe is $\beta = 0$. 125 124

The failure being fought has moved. With 20+ turn trajectories, async rollouts and mismatched training and inference engines, drift from the sampler matters more than drift from the starting model. 126 127 "In early RHF it was like training reference and now in RLVR for agents it's like sampler trainer". 128 The TMax talk shows that gap, "the trainer generator gap in asynchronous RL training". 129

The new tools act on the trust region. DPPO masks tokens by a directly estimated divergence between the rollout and training policies, instead of PPO's per-token ratio clip; GLM's IcePop and Kimi's log-ratio interval do the same job: "gradients masked, not clipped". 130 131 In TMax, DPPO "increases the stability of this RL run". 132 The slide: "This is where the eye of regularization is today". 130 Lambert adds "this thing is still pretty new for me". 133 Clipping and the trust region are on the Chapter 6 page.

Check yourself

Which direction is the KL penalty, and how is it estimated?

Reverse KL to the reference: sample from the policy, score under the reference. 9 22

Why is "KL distance" technically wrong?

KL is asymmetric, so it is not a metric. People say "distance" for how far the model moved. 35 37

In Ziegler et al.'s controller, what happens to β when KL is above target?

The error $e_t$ is positive, so β grows and pulls the policy back. 48

In the book's code, does a GRPO run with beta: 0.1 go through apply_reward_kl?

No. That function only applies to PPO, RLOO and REINFORCE; GRPO's KL goes in the loss. 55 58

Why is minimizing the SFT loss the same as minimizing forward KL?

The forward KL splits into the data's negative entropy, constant in $\theta$, plus the SFT loss: same gradients, same minimum. 82

What happens to the reward-tilted reference as β shrinks?

The tilt $e^{r/\beta}$ grows, so mass concentrates on the highest-reward completions. 87

Mode-seeking RL should forget more. Why doesn't it?

That assumes a unimodal policy. With an old and a new mode, forward KL pulls mass from the old mode, while reverse KL moves the new mode and leaves the old one alone. 15 107

According to RL's Razor, what explains RL's smaller KL drift?

On-policy data; negative gradients had no discernible effect. 17
