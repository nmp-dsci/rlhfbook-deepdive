Chapter 10: The Nature of Preferences

RLHF exists for problems where writing a reward function is hard, and human preferences are what it models instead. 1 This chapter asks what a preference is, and how economics, psychology, philosophy and other fields shaped the tools for measuring and optimizing one. 2 Its argument: assumptions inherited from economics, philosophy and optimal control "often do not apply in practice". 3 There is no math to derive here and no code. It is the conceptual chapter, and Lambert calls its problems "the lasting problem of RLHF". 4

The chapter in five points

  1. Preferences replace correctness. When there is no right answer, as with "which poem is better?", you ask people to compare. 5 6
  2. RLHF borrows from three fields. Philosophy and economics, optimal control and RL, and deep learning, each with its own idea of what a preference is. 7 8
  3. Costs, rewards and preferences are different things. RLHF treats them as interchangeable. 9 10
  4. The tools assume a stable, single reward. RL's guarantees hold for a closed-form reward; human preferences drift and do not compress into one number. 11 12 13
  5. The theory that licenses a scalar reward does not hold here. The VNM theorem allows a utility function only if preferences meet axioms they break in practice. 14 15

After this chapter you should be able to

  • Explain why RLHF collects comparisons rather than demonstrations or scores.
  • Name the three fields RLHF draws on, and what each assumes about a preference.
  • Say what the VNM theorem requires, and which requirements RLHF data breaks.
  • Explain why a single scalar reward cannot represent some groups' preferences.
  • Distinguish the two meanings of "value" in RLHF papers.

When preference replaces correctness

In Lecture 8: 0:32 · 1:05 · 2:09 · 2:39

The framing question: "how do we hill climb on capabilities we don't really know how to score". 16 17 The answer RLHF took is that it is easier to judge between a good and a bad example than to write a good one. 18 In 2023 Lambert called this "the fundamental assumption" RLHF is built on. 19

The chapter's test: on February 26, 2025, Lambert asked Claude 3.7 Sonnet and ChatGPT with GPT-4o for a short poem about an optimistic goldfish. 20

Which is better, and which model wrote which? Compare that with "Who is the president of the United States?", which has a right answer. 5 In the lecture Lambert admits he no longer knows which model wrote which. Ask many people and "you will get a distribution of answers". 23 Preferences are what you reach for when there is no right answer, 24 and preference tuning "has been very effective at improving capabilities". 25

Modern RLHF is the meeting point of three areas: philosophy, psychology, economics and decision theory; optimal control and RL; and deep learning. 7 26 Each brings its own assumptions about what a preference is. In practice RLHF is studied as empirical alignment, maximizing skills, not as calibration to values. 8 Work on "pluralistic alignment" across populations continues, though. 27

The origins of RLHF and preferences

In Lecture 8: 4:13 · 4:44 · 7:48 · 8:19

The chapter, and the first half of the lecture, follow a 2023 paper Lambert wrote with two colleagues, The History and Risks of Reinforcement Learning and Human Feedback. 28 It traces RLHF back through the fields it borrows from and asks "what breaks in the borrowing". Its main charge is that RLHF treats costs, rewards and preferences as interchangeable, and inherits RL's optimizers without their convergence guarantees. 29

Everything rests on the assumption that human preferences exist, which goes back to Aristotle's Topics. 41 42 Philosophy is the oldest branch, and language models are "deeply kind of imbued with philosophical thinking". 43 Problem specifications have since kept moving away from domains where optimal solutions exist, toward under-specified models used as approximations. 44

Aside: objective mismatch

Lambert brings in the idea from his PhD in model-based RL: a dynamics model is trained for prediction accuracy, not control, so a "better" model can give a worse policy. RLHF repeats this one level up. A reward model is trained for classification accuracy, not for the quality of the policy it trains, so reward-model accuracy is "a proxy for a proxy". 45 The best reward model "is not necessarily the one that is the most accurate on your training data". 46 47 The over-optimization lecture calls it "just an objective mismatch". 48

Specifying objectives: from the logic of utility to reward functions

In Lecture 8: 8:50 · 9:21 · 9:52

RLHF optimizes only against reward models, so it assumes preferences, rewards and costs converge. 9 The goal is "preferences as a single number which we can optimize". 49 The idea that wants reduce to one measure is old: 50

  • 1662, the Port Royal Logic: weigh the good or evil of an outcome by "the probability that it happens or does not happen". 31
  • Bentham's hedonic calculus: everything in life could be weighed. 32
  • 1931, Ramsey's Truth and Probability: the first quantitative application, 32 quantifying preference and belief together. 50

The debates were rarely settled in theory; subfields reached their own consensus instead. 51 A minority of economists hold that preferences are too hard to measure, because people have preferences over their own and others' preferences. That view "is not reflected in the RLHF process". 52 Some behavioral economists go further: preferences may not exist, and may be just a methodological tool. 53

The other half of the vocabulary came from elsewhere. "Reward" comes from operant conditioning and the Law of Effect: a scale of how good an action is. 54 Utility-to-go came from control theory, in analog circuits in 1960, built for systems with clear optimality. 33 Those assumptions "stand in sharp contrast to reward models that aggregate multimodal preferences". 55

The chapter quotes Singh and colleagues on what a reward is in RL: 56

Rewards in an RL system correspond to primary rewards, i.e., rewards that in animals have been hard-wired by the evolutionary process due to their relevance to reproductive success.

Lambert's reading: a value function approximates the true reward of an environment, and the confusion "just reinforces why all of this is so messy". 57 RL uses rewards to steer toward clearly defined goals, which "is in conflict when many individual desires are reduced to a single function". 58

Tools for optimizing utility

In Lecture 8: 15:01 · 15:32 · 16:04 · 17:07 · 17:38

RL grew out of dynamic programming and optimal control: the Bellman equation, solved inside an MDP, before RL existed as a field. 59 The term "reinforcement learning" came from psychology in the 1960s, and Klopf brought back trial-and-error learning. 60 TD learning and Q-learning followed, 61 then deep RL with DQN and AlphaZero. 36 The catch: these guarantees "have only been demonstrated for domains cast as MDPs" or tasks with a single closed-form reward. 11 36 Most reward or cost functions imply an optimal behavior; "models of human preferences cannot". 62

Lambert sums up the pitch behind RLHF: model preferences as one function, and use the tools that solve closed-form functions. "But in reality there are cost functions, reward functions and preference functions and all of these are very different." 63

From the slides: 10 treating preferences as rewards made the optimization tractable, but is "the root cause" of many of RLHF's unsolvable biases. A learned reward model is "a moving, noisy proxy", not a ground-truth reward, 64 which is why over-optimization happens. 65

Inverse RL, learning a reward from behavior, is close to reward modeling but rarely mentioned in RLHF. The chapter puts that down to the engineering path RLHF took, and argues for more comparison with it. 66 64 Lambert's view of IRL has shifted over time; see where the sources differ.

The complexity of optimizing preferences

In Lecture 8: 10:22 · 10:52 · 11:24 · 11:54 · 12:24 · 12:55 · 13:25 · 14:30

RL assumes rewards and costs are stable functions: the same state and action, the same return. Human preferences "constantly drift". 12 The word "value" adds confusion. In RL it is a numerical estimate of future reward; in alignment it is a moral principle. 67 A reward model maps text to a scalar while missing dynamics like preference shift over a labeling session. At best it compresses "a multi-dimensional reward landscape into a single scalar function". 13 68

The VNM theorem, and why it does not hold

In theory, the Von Neumann-Morgenstern utility theorem licenses a scalar reward. 14 It is "the result RLHF leans on". 15 Lambert calls it the theorem that let optimal control and RL be applied to human preferences, 69 and the economics behind "maximize expected utility" in an intro AI class. 70 Its conditions fail in RLHF: 15 71

VNM assumesRLHF data shows
Stable preferencesThey drift during and after labeling, with time, mood and experience 15 72
Preferences independent of presentationFraming and display change them 73 74
TransitivityAt high complexity they can be intransitive 15 75
One utilityMany dimensions squashed into one number 15 68

"In like one narrow narrow case they might" hold, Lambert says, but not in general. 76 The reduction "is what makes this tractable, but is kind of the source of so many trade-offs". 77 The MDP can in theory be adapted to the VNM theorem, but rarely is. 78

Bradley-Terry (1952) is the model that turns comparisons into a single score. 79 It became the backbone of reward modeling, and it is "where the imperfections enter". 80 There are infinitely many ways to teach an annotator to pick between two completions, "so it's impossible to do perfectly". 81

Aggregating people

  • Choice is not preference. Sen: revealed preference may recapitulate Hume's guillotine, and "what do I want?" differs from "is X better than Y?". 82
  • Impossibility. Social choice theorems show no preference optimization technique meets every fairness criterion at once. 83 35 Assuming utilities can be compared between people avoids this, but the result may conflict with corrigibility. 84
  • Change over time. Preferences change, and aggregating across people, places and situations is "central to modern RLHF dataset engineering". 85

Social choice, the field that studies aggregation, is "a natural lens for alignment": whose preferences count, and how do you combine disagreeing labelers? 86

Where theory and practice part

The chapter ends with four ways the assumptions fail in practice: 75

  • preferences may not be transitive or comparable as the setting gets more complex;
  • proxy signals from implicit data, such as time on page or a repeated question, are used without asking how they interact with training and deployment;
  • the number and presentation of options change the results;
  • low agreement between respondents can hide contextual differences that the reward model averages away.

Lambert adds that static data collection was "always" going to be a limitation, because preferences are not stable. 87 72

Open questions

In Lecture 8: 33:11 · 33:44 · 34:14 · 34:46

The lecture closes Chapters 10 and 11 with questions that apply here:

  • Who labels, and is disagreement noise or signal? Paid or volunteer, workplace or end user. 88 Lambert does not expect AI models to "ever be democratic". 89
  • Are the preferences even expressed in the model? 90 RLHF's motivation has drifted from aligning to human preference to making models effective. Because industry RLHF is closed, no one can check whether a model reflects the spec given to annotators. 91 "This type of audit was never done publicly". 92 He asked the same in 2023: "no one knows everyone does it but it's never talked about". 93
  • Should everything be averaged? "so much of ml is built over averaging". 94

He calls it "a good academic problem": well motivated, with literature to build on, and not crowded. 95 96

Check yourself

Why does RLHF ask labelers to compare two answers rather than write the ideal one?

Judging is easier than generating for many tasks, especially without a right answer, like a poem. 19 18

What does the VNM theorem promise, and on what condition?

If preferences meet axioms like completeness, transitivity, continuity and independence, a single utility function represents them, and rational choice maximizes expected utility. 15

Name two ways RLHF preference data breaks those conditions.

Any two of: drift over time and while labeling, dependence on framing and display, intransitivity, and many dimensions squashed into one. 15 73 12

Why can't a Bradley-Terry reward model represent three equal groups with cyclic rankings?

It gives each answer one score, so its preferences are always transitive. A cycle has no scalar representation, so the fit settles on equal scores. That is the chapter's "may not be transitive" problem. 75 13

What is objective mismatch in RLHF?

The reward model is trained for classification accuracy, but what you want is a good policy after training against it. The most accurate reward model need not be the most useful. 45 46

Costs, rewards and preferences: where does each come from?

Costs from control, rewards from psychology and RL, and preferences from people, relational and unstable. 10

Agent notes

agent note The cycle in the widget is a Condorcet cycle. Not Lambert. Three voters with rankings $A \succ B \succ C$, $B \succ C \succ A$ and $C \succ A \succ B$ give 2 to 1 majorities for $A$ over $B$, $B$ over $C$ and $C$ over $A$. Bradley-Terry models $P(i \succ j) = \sigma(r_i - r_j)$, so whenever it predicts $i$ over $j$ and $j$ over $k$, it predicts $i$ over $k$. It cannot fit a cycle. The maximum-likelihood fit here sets all three scores equal. The chapter cites the impossibility theorems; 83 this is the simplest case of the problem, not the theorem.

agent note Pooling hides groups. Not Lambert. Make one group much larger in the widget and the fitted scores follow it closely. The smaller groups' rankings then show up only as a few points of shortfall in the pairwise fit. That matches the chapter's last point, that low agreement can mask contextual differences the model aggregates "without resolving". 75
