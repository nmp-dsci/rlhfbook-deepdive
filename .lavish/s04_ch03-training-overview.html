Chapter 3: Training Overview

Chapter 3 is the map for the rest of the book. It gives you one objective: maximize a learned proxy for human preferences, with a penalty for moving too far from the starting model. It then gives you the recipes that use it, which later chapters keep pointing back to. 1

The chapter in five points

  1. Standard RL. An agent acts, the environment responds, and the agent maximizes discounted reward over a trajectory. 2
  2. RLHF prunes that setup down. A learned model replaces the environment's reward, state transitions go away, and a whole response gets one undiscounted reward. 3 4 5 Lambert calls this "pretty contrived", and says the magic is that RL's methods still work. 6
  3. A penalty protects what the model already knows. RLHF starts from a strong pretrained model, 7 so it adds a KL penalty, and $\beta$ sets how far the model may move. 8
  4. RLHF is one tool among several. Modern RLHF-trained models start with instruction fine-tuning, then use a mix of reward models, rejection sampling, policy gradients and direct alignment. 9 10
  5. The recipes keep changing. InstructGPT's three steps gave way to Tülu 3, DeepSeek R1 and specialist recipes merged with MOPD. The trend is more steps, more algorithms and more diverse data, 11 with compute shifting toward RLVR. 12

After this chapter you should be able to

  • Write the RL objective and point to what RLHF removes from it.
  • Explain each term of the RLHF objective, and which way $\beta$ moves the model.
  • Say which later chapter teaches each post-training tool.
  • Sketch the InstructGPT, Tülu 3, DeepSeek R1 and MOPD recipes, and say what changed each time.

Standard RL

In Lecture 1: 15:38 · 16:08 · 17:11

An agent samples actions from a policy, given the state of the environment, to maximize reward. 14 The lecture's short version is trial and error, balancing exploration and exploitation. 15 Two equations carry the setup.

A trajectory's probability multiplies the start state, every action the policy chooses and every transition the environment makes: 16

$$p_{\pi}(\tau)=\rho_0(s_0)\prod_{t=0}^{T-1}\pi(a_t\mid s_t)\,p(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t)$$

The goal is the policy with the highest expected discounted reward across those trajectories: 2

$$\max_\pi \; \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim p_{\pi}} \left[ \sum_{t=0}^{T-1} \gamma^t r(s_t, a_t) \right]$$
TermMeaning
$\rho_0(s_0)$The initial state distribution. 16
$\pi(a_t\mid s_t)$The policy: maps each state to a probability distribution over actions. 14
$p(s_{t+1}\mid s_t,a_t)$The transition: how the environment moves to its next state. 16
$\gamma$The discount, from 0 to 1: balances near-term against future rewards. 2
$T$The horizon. For continuing tasks, $T\to\infty$ and $\gamma<1$ keeps the objective well-defined. 2
$J(\pi)$The usual name for the expected return of a policy. 2

CartPole is the chapter's richer example, with four continuous state variables and physics-based transitions. 20 The point the lecture draws from it is that classical RL is a multi-step control problem, 21 which is exactly what RLHF gives up. Its update equations are left out here.

Manipulating the setup: what RLHF changes

In Lecture 1: 22:54 · 23:24

RLHF sets a few pieces of RL to specific definitions to fit language models. 23 There are three changes:

  1. A reward model replaces the reward function. This gives the designer more flexibility and control over the result, at the cost of implementation complexity. 3
  2. No state transitions. A prompt comes from a dataset, the completion is the action, and those two make a complete episode. 4
  3. One reward per response, with no discounting. The reward covers a whole sequence of tokens, which is why this is sometimes called a bandit problem. Implementations usually set $\gamma = 1$ so the algorithm sees every token as part of the same action. 5

With one step and a learned reward, the time horizon and discount drop out of the objective: 24

$$\max_\pi \; \mathbb{E}_{\tau \sim \pi} \left[r_\theta(s_t, a_t) \right]$$

The lecture is blunter than the chapter: there is no environment, the model generates a completion and stops, and it is surprising that methods built for RL still work once the setup is pruned this far. 6

Two things to keep straight

  • The reward is a proxy. At its best, the reward model only correlates with user satisfaction. 25 26 Chapter 14 covers what happens when you optimize it too hard.
  • A response-level reward can still train a token-level loss. A reader asked why anyone would choose token-level over sequence-level rewards. Lambert answered that these are two kinds of granularity: rewards are not getting much finer, but outcome-based rewards are used to get a token-level loss in the implementation. 27

Fine-tuning and regularization

In Lecture 1: 23:24 · 27:36 · 28:06

Standard RL starts from a random policy. RLHF starts from a strong pretrained model, and that strong prior creates a need to stop the optimization drifting too far. 7 In early RLHF work, the lecture says, changing the model too much degraded it into "total yapping". 30 The most common fix is a KL penalty: 8

$$\max_{\pi} \; \mathbb{E}_{x \sim D,\; y \sim \pi(\cdot \mid x)} \underbrace{r_\phi(x, y)}_{\text{maximize the reward}} - \underbrace{\beta D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(\pi(\cdot \mid x)\,\|\,\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}(\cdot \mid x)\right)}_{\text{but don't change the model too much}}$$

This form is from the slides. 31 The chapter writes the same objective with $r_\theta(s_t,a_t)$. 8

TermMeaning
$x \sim D$A prompt from the dataset. 32
$y \sim \pi(\cdot\mid x)$A completion generated by the model being trained. 17
$r_\phi(x,y)$The learned reward model's score for that completion. 31
$\beta$The penalty's strength. A larger $\beta$ keeps the model closer to its start, and a smaller one gives more freedom to chase reward. 8
$D_{\mathrm{KL}}$How far the new policy moves from the reference on this prompt. 31 Not a true distance: see below. 33
$\pi_{\mathrm{ref}}$The reference model, normally the SFT checkpoint from earlier in the recipe, 34 held frozen. 35
  • Think of it as a budget. A lot of RLHF study goes into how to spend a "KL budget". Chapter 15 goes further. 36
  • It is the main defense, and it is not complete. The slides call $\beta$ the main defense against over-optimization, which remains a fundamental tension in preference-based training. 26
  • It costs you in systems. You keep a reference model alongside the trained model and the reward model, and the lecture calls the system side very hard. 37 The 2025 talk describes the same thing: the agent has a few language models inside it. 38 This is what the chapter's opening means by linking multiple models together. 39
  • RLVR often drops it. For basic math and code RLVR, Lambert says it seems you do not need a KL penalty, because verifiable answers avoid the same over-optimization problem, though this is getting messier with agentic models. 40 41 Page s01 answers this question in full. In the 2025 talk, RLVR runs at AI2 showed very low KL. 42

Subtle advantages of RL

Rejection sampling and DPO are far simpler than getting RL working, yet RL-based methods continue to win out, and the updates "generally help the model a lot". The chapter says this is hard to quantify and gives two forms: 50

  • RL can "fix" rough edges. The model becomes easier to chat with or more robust. The exact reason is not well known. 51
  • RL can be surgical. It tends not to "squash" general capabilities. The chapter's example is Tülu 3. 52 The 2025 talk puts this as stability: RL as fine-tuning does not degrade things out of distribution. 53

Q&A 3 takes the harder version: if RL is basically SFT with regularization, why not just SFT? 54 Lambert gives two reasons. RL generates on-policy experience that you cannot produce ahead of time, 55 and it has negative gradients, so it also shows the model what not to do. 56 Lambert also says more models could get by with just SFT than people think, especially small ones. 57

The price is engineering. The Olmo 3 talk says of RL infrastructure, "this is very hard. Do not take this lightly", 58 and that three to four people spent their entire summer on it. 59

Canonical training recipes

In Lecture 1: 31:42 · 32:43 · 33:46

What each one added

  • InstructGPT: the template. Around ChatGPT's launch this was the accepted method, with RLHF at the centre. 60 The lecture calls it "a bit outdated", but the simplest way to get to RLHF, 66 and probably directionally what the original ChatGPT used. 67
  • Tülu 3: open, and with RLVR. A fully open recipe where RLHF plays a major role. 62 It has been applied to Llama 3.1, OLMo 2 and SmolLM. 68 The lecture calls it a mature version of the InstructGPT recipe, 69 and Olmo 3 uses a similar three stages, SFT then DPO then RL. 70
  • DeepSeek R1: RL moves earlier. Reasoning models re-ordered the stages and redistributed compute, and R1 is the clearest documented case. 71 Large-scale RL comes before the general SFT stage, and the last stage mixes verifiable rewards with preference reward models. 63
  • MOPD: specialists, then a merge. The move to coding agents came with MOPD, which merges diverse skills into one model, while RL compute kept growing. The chapter says these recipes have less depth and more breadth. 64 Reported counts include more than ten experts for Nemotron 3 Ultra and nine for Kimi K3. 72 Labs are far from unanimous, though: GLM-5 runs a three-stage RL process before MOPD. 73 Q&A 3 sums up what is happening now: sequential by optimizer, parallel by domain. 74 Lecture 1 does not cover this, so go to Chapter 12 75 and Lecture 7.

Check yourself

Map the thermostat onto RLHF. What plays the state and the action, and what has no counterpart?

The prompt is the state and the completion is the action. 4 The room's physics has no counterpart, because a completion does not define the next prompt. 17

Why set $\gamma = 1$ in RLHF when standard RL uses $\gamma < 1$?

The reward covers the whole completion, so there is no sequence of later rewards to balance against earlier ones. Setting $\gamma = 1$ helps the algorithm see every token as part of the same action. 5

Reward keeps climbing, but the text degrades into repetition. Which way do you move $\beta$, and why might that not be enough?

Increase it, since a larger $\beta$ keeps the model closer to its start. 8 Changing the model too much is what degraded early RLHF models. 30 It may not be enough, because the reward model is a proxy and over-optimization remains a fundamental tension. 26

If RL's loss can look like SFT's with a regularizer, what does RL give you that SFT cannot?

Experience generated from the current model, which you cannot produce ahead of time, 55 and negative gradients that show the model what not to do. 56

A MOPD recipe and DeepSeek R1 both show four stages in Figure 2. Is their complexity the same kind?

No. The chapter says MOPD recipes have less depth in iterative model versions, but more breadth, because they need many specialized experts. 64

Olmo 3's SFT set has a couple million prompts, and Tülu 3's has about one million. Is that about twice the training?

No. Olmo 3's set is about 20 times the tokens, because of long thinking traces. 83 62 This is why teams balance by flops rather than prompts. 85
